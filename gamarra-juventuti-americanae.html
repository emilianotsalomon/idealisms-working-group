JUVENTUTI AMERICANAE

Juan Benito Díaz de Gamarra sends greetings and his lucubrations.

Jo. Bened. Diaz Gamarra S. vigiliasque.

Your cause, all of you, is at stake, young men of most excellent talent, fitted beyond other nations for the Sciences. It is your cause that is now at stake. For I did not wish these pages to be written for, or dedicated to, learned Men who think they have attained Philosophy, such as it is, by the labor of two years (and not very strenuous labor at that); nor to those who all day long pronounce from the School’s lectern, as from a tripod, on matters exceedingly difficult and not equally well known to themselves (*). But to you, young men, who are now for the first time approaching these sacred rites, and who are about to enter, when at length it may be allowed, the citadel of human Philosophy, than which nothing can be more delightful.

Vestra omnium res agitur, Juvenes ingenio praestantissimi, & praeter ceteras gentes ad Scientias comparati. Vestra nunc res agitur. For if you do not know what Philosophy is, under what Authors it was born, how far and in what esteem it was held among our forebears, what its fortunes were, what its progress, what its way and method of teaching, it cannot possibly be that you will not err shamefully at every turn. And no kind of error, in my opinion at least, is more to be guarded against and avoided than that of those for whom this alone counts as true philosophy, what they themselves learned as beardless boys; everything else they hold as nothing, as though it were the empty dreams of a sick man’s fancy. As for St. Thomas (for of all the others I must speak afterwards), no mortal ever embraced more, none more skillfully, more learnedly, more temperately, none more briefly, all the things that seemed to bear upon Sacred Theology, or could in any way be referred to it. Immortal God! what genius the Man had! what abundance in disputation! how constant, how great his diligence in unrolling the volumes of the Sacred Page! how he revered the Holy Fathers of the Church! how incredible and almost divine that acumen of his altogether Angelic mind in discerning the most difficult things! what candor in writing, what method in teaching, what severity in judging! You might truly say (as Cicero had said before, in a matter not very unlike) that the books of St. Thomas Aquinas, and especially that Theological Summa, though all should roar against it, far and greatly surpass the libraries of all the Theologians, both in the abundance of the greatest matters and in the strength of authority. Putidi enim vanique sunt.

Quòd si postremo id loco nobis objecerint, frustrà in lucem haec decerpta nostra edi in gratiam Juventutis, quum aliae Institutiones non desint, antehac typis evulgatae: ostendant ij, quaeso, ubinam tandem reperire sit (quod diu multumque quaesivimus) doctrinam recentiorem, more ab Scholis omninò non abhorrente, & cùm brevissimè traditam tùm planam illam satis, apertam, ordine congestam, adornatam, atque (id quod maximum est) utilem futuram in reliqua vita (*).

Bacon, Arnauld, Descartes, Gassendi, Malebranche, Locke, Heineccius, Wolff, Genovesi, Verney he holds of no account, as if they had not advanced Logic themselves but only treated it perfunctorily. Aristotle alone is esteemed great and famous, after whom Piquer alone handed down the precepts of Logic; the rest have all too often inculcated mere trifles, and things not at all to the point. So great indeed was the most distinguished man’s philautia (let him tell us himself, in his usual way, what the force of that word is in Latin, Greek and Spanish), and his preconceived opinion in favor of antiquity, that he has roused that venerable Old Man, the ornament of our now aging century and of our Spain, lately gone to his fate, Fr. Benito Jerónimo Feijóo, whom he holds up to ridicule on the ground that he had praised some of the Moderns named above without discrimination. [PDF 13] where truer things will not be at hand. Detractors of this stamp, querulous, difficult, more frivolous even than anyone could imagine men of mature mind to be, whom riding on a reed, and nuts, and odd-and-even no longer delight: these, I say, do not listen to. For they are tiresome and empty.

But if, finally, they object to us that it is useless for these extracts of ours to be published for the benefit of Youth, since other Institutions, already made public in print, are not lacking: let them show, I beg, where at length one may find (what we have long and much sought) a newer doctrine, in a manner not at all at odds with the Schools, and both very briefly delivered and also sufficiently plain, open, arranged in order, adorned, and (what matters most) likely to be useful in the rest of life (*).
