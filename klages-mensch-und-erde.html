The five treatises which appear here in book form under the title of the first arose in the course of the last seven years on various occasions and, with the exception of the fourth, have also already been published in periodicals: the first in the Festschrift of the Free German Youth for the centenary celebration on the Hoher Meißner, the second and third in the „Münchner Allgemeine Zeitung“, the fifth in the monthly „Die Schweiz“. The fourth, finally, is taken from a sequence of personal letters of instruction. — The author's decision to offer them to the public once more, united in one volume, was occasioned chiefly by the perception that the "world-view", whose light they unfold into single colours, is, given its extraordinary opposition to the habits of thinking most favoured today, condemned either not to be apprehended at all or to be misunderstood, if it comes into effect solely for the solution of particular questions. Every age, and ours above all, has its catchwords, with which it proclaims its tendencies as with rolls of drums, deafening the voice of doubt in the ranks of its adherents and rallying ever new troops from among the impartial around its banner. The three strongest of today's run "progress", "culture" and "personality", yet in such a way that the thought of progress, as alone peculiar to the present, carries the two others and lends them their characteristic colour in the prevailing thinking. The present, then, believes itself superior to the primitive peoples no less than to the periods of history that preceded it, and to the question on what it grounds this it has the answer ready: science stands at a height never reached before, "technology" masters nature, before which every earlier mankind drew back at a loss, from the inexhaustible stores of the earth it feeds the general welfare according to plan, spirit penetrates space and time with the far-speaking wave of the ether, and even the boundless sea of the air has now at last been "conquered" by its inventive genius. Not for the convinced confessors of this faith, who will die with it, but for a younger generation that still asks, we shall try, at least at one point, to lift the veil and to uncover the menacing self-deception which it conceals.

To the ancient Hellene the most highly desired was "kalokagathia", that is, the inner and outer beauty of man, which he saw in the image of the Olympians; to the Middle Ages the "salvation of the soul", by which it understood the spiritual elevation to God; to Goethean man the perfection of bearing, "mastery" amid the changes of fortune; and, however different such goals, we understand without further ado the deep happiness in the attainment of each one. But what the progressive is proud of are mere successes, are increases in the power of mankind, which he thoughtlessly confuses with increases in value, and we must doubt whether he is capable of appreciating a happiness and does not rather know only the empty satisfaction which the consciousness of mastery gives. Power alone is indeed blind to all values, blind to truth and right and, where it must still admit these, quite certainly blind to beauty and life. In our counter-reckoning we start from what is well known.

The majority of our contemporaries, penned together in great cities and accustomed from youth on to smoking chimneys, the din of street noise and nights bright as day, no longer have any measure for the beauty of the landscape, believe they are already seeing nature at the sight of a potato field, and find even higher claims satisfied when a few starlings and sparrows twitter in the meagre trees along the highway. But if, for once, from the sounding and the fragrance of the German landscape as it still was some seventy years ago, a breath from the words and images of those days should touch the desolated souls, there are soon enough weatherproof phrases again about "economic development", the requirements of "utility", the unavoidable necessities of the cultural process, to banish the admonishing reproach. Here, then, it is necessary that we widen somewhat the circle of our consideration.

If the present has grown dull to the wonder-working of elevated speech in general, it has lost altogether every relation to the artistic perfection and the breath-filled calm of the epic poet. Fragmented and devoid of intuition as it is, it will at any rate sooner acquire a taste for sentimental traits and know how to value iridescent single beauties. Therefore let it finally be

mentioned that over Jordan's poetry there lies the mood of an almost impersonal melancholy, which, in contrast to his progressive conviction, almost unceasingly recalls the eternal leave-taking of all life. And as the tear in the eye of a man is more moving, so the shy and rare sounds in which the most personal sorrow of a soul otherwise bound by its destiny to silence laments move us more inwardly than the luxuriating effusions of feeling of the "lyricists". Although the "Strophen und Stäbe" offer thoughts and reflections for three quarters of their length, a single poem such as this one suffices to secure for its creator an imperishable place even among the post-Goethean masters of song.

Wie voll Hast / Alles rennt! / Für die Rast / Kein Moment! / Glücklos fühlt sich, wer ein Glück in seiner Macht hat.

How full of haste / All things run! / For rest / Not a moment! / Unhappy feels he who has a happiness in his power.

Unverweilt / Sonnenfern / Wieder eilt / Unser Stern / Wann zur Nähe seinen Zirkel er vollbracht hat.

Unlingering, / Far from the sun, / Again hastens / Our star, / When it has completed its circle toward nearness.

Wie so bald / Kühler Herbst, / Du den Wald / Gelb entfärbst / Der kaum fertig seine sommergrüne Tracht hat!

How so soon, / Cool autumn, / You the wood / Discolour to yellow, / That has scarcely finished its summer-green garb!
