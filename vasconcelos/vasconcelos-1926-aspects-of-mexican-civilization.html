THE LATIN-AMERICAN BASIS OF MEXICAN CIVILIZATION By José Vasconcelos

SIMILARITY AND CONTRAST

Many travelers have recorded the same impression; even casual observers have seen that Mexico's most striking feature is the extreme difference in the appearance of the inhabitants, in the nature of the soil, in the climate of nearby sections, abyssmal differences in the thought of the people and in the landscape of the country. Perhaps there is no other nation on earth where you can find, in the same accentuated form, a coexistence of human types separated by centuries and even by epochs of ethnographical development—peopledifferentin blood, race tradition, and habits. And such plurality of types is not a new condition that might be attributed to the Spanish invasion alone, because long before the Spaniards landed, a mixture of peoples existed, and the Spaniards only contributed one more race-wave in the already mixed waters of our national plasma. Remember that archaeologists tell us there is no connecting link between the race that built the Teotihuacan pyramids and the Aztecs of the day of Montezuma. The Aztecs themselves were invaders and comparative newcomers

p. 4

who had overwhelmed the Chichimecs, and then the Chichimec on his part, had received practically no inheritance, at least no direct influence, from the builders of the ancient palaces. Also there is no known relation between any of the successive civilizations of the plateau and the wonder-builders of the Maya-Quiché hot contries of Guatemala, Yucatan, and Tabasco-the race that has left such a magnificent testimony of its genius in the monuments whose silent messages have not yet been deciphered.

All countries, you may say, all old countries, show the landmarks of this uninterrupted work of history through which races arise, prosper, and then decay only to be overpowered and replaced by newcomers; but the one fact I wish to emphasize is the fact that Mexico is a land where the process of history has been one of continuous destruction and substitution of cultures instead of the regular growing and evolving of one period into the other. With constantly alternated and disconnected efforts of enlightenment, instead of a continuous cumulative progression, the Mexico of today still shows trace of the multiplied experience of our history. Independent and even conflicting manners of culture have developed in our land and sometimes have become enrooted in it; but suddenly they are driven

p. 5

away, or they disappear through decay, before they are able to evolve into a higher civilization; they are like a stem giving out a beautiful flower only to die without leaving a seed. And thus the soil remains barren until another accidental flora, independent of the preceding one, comes in turn to life. Instead of an evolutionary series of events, as you may find for instance in the history of Europe, the history of Mexico will show you a number of glorious but lonely, disconnected efforts like sparks in the darkness, and in the background a constantly recurring tragedy of destruction and anarchy. In this manner even the wise of every period find themselves unable to take advantages of the cultural conquests of the preceding epoch; and each new period through the destruction of the past finds itself in the need of building anew the whole fabric of social life. Such an overwhelming undertaking consumes, of course, the national energy and postpones the realization of truly transcendent plans.

A series of layers composed of materials that do not mix-such is the sketch of our history. A compound of races that have not yet become thoroughly combined-such is the social condition of Mexico, notwithstanding that four hundred years ago, the Spaniards, although introducing themselves as a new element of complexity, still brought

p. 6

forth the first organized effort to blend the different peoples into one faith, one rule, one purpose. The blending process is yet unfinished either because human nature opposes such tendencies as are imposed upon it through force or because the conflicting elements are so deeply differentiated that even religion has found the task a hard and lengthy one.

Let us now set aside for a moment the racial question in order to consider the nature of the soil. A glance at our physical structure will only add to the puzzle of the Mexican problem. A sort of elongated, immensely vast pyramid, cut at the top end, then sloping toward the two seas. On the top a temperate climate fit for the pine, the wheat, and the corn, and as you go down, all the varieties of plants and of animals, together with the unexplored unlimited wealth of the tropical jungle. All climates and all resources, just as amongst the people you will find all sorts of faces-the white man's face represented by the gallego and the asturiano of modern Spain and by the descendants of the old Castilian and the gay Andalusian—the oldtime gentlemen and landowners who are gradually absorbed in blood and in temperament by the ascending indigenous wave of the mestizo and the Indian. Standards of living also multiply and co-

p. 7

exist, from wealth and refinement to absolute des: titution and want. A country of caste and oppositions of every sort, a country of worry and of wonder. Even in respect to moral temperament our lot varies from the saintly deeds of the missionaries to the wretched cruelties of the conquerors, and in latter days from the sternness and enlightenment of a Juarez or a Madero to the brutality of military chieftains who impose political murder as the rule of government. Such is our Mexico, a country of hope and despair; the rhythm of its very life is the rhythm of contrast.

The nation where contrast is the rule instead of the exception—such is, I believe, the main trait of our national being. Perhaps the reason of the social contrast lies in the varied physical, temperamental, and historical factors that intervene in the making of our soul. Progress in other lands seems to be a comparatively easy affair, instead of a permanent rhythm of contradictions; a well-regulated series of efforts; an addition of similar elements in order to constitute progress; a constant flow of cumulations instead of the repeated subtractions in which we lose ourselves. For instance—the original United States, with its level, rich soil, all about the same for cultivating purposes from New England to Georgia and to the Middle West; a land open to the

p. 8

flow of civilization; the tool fit for one place, being simply moved along in increased rate of production. Compare that with Mexico where the climate and the soil and the kind of the tillage change from north to south, from east to west, and within only a few miles around any given spot. Compare also the homogeneous racial stock at the beginning of this country, the unmixed European of the original Eastern colonies, developing in a virgin land; compare that with the hundred different stocks that inhabit the Mexican territory, and you will then understand why it is that our country has not been able to give a crop —it has not had the time necessary to ripen its fruit.

But let us say from the beginning that the case of Mexico is not an isolated fact, a national situation; the case of Mexico is a portion of a larger continental situation. The case of Mexico is exactly the same as the case of Bolivia and Peru, of Chile and Venezuela and Central America. In every one of these nations physical conditions and traditions combine to produce exactly the same results. In order to be more exact in our views it will then be necessary to abandon for a moment the nationalistic pattern, which is of a very superficial mold, in order to study the Latin-American civilization in accordance with such natural subdivisions

p. 9

as spring to sight as soon as we reflect on the question. Take, for instance, the type of culture that is to be found in Peru, Colombia, or Mexico and compare it with the more simple social manner of Argentina or Uruguay and you will have a clear start in the judgment of our problems.

Let us speak, then, of a certain whole composed of the high plateau section that comprehends Mexico, Central America, Colombia, Venezuela, Peru, Ecuador, Bolivia, and Chile. Let us also imagine as one continuous portion all of the lowland occupied by Argentina, Uruguay, and Brazil. The moment we grasp the contrast of these two geographically defined sections of our continent we are in a position to judge from a point of view of reality far more significant than by reference to the political subdivisions. Let us insist upon the outline of these two natural divisions of our America, and let us connect them with a view of the northern section of the New World. We shall have, then, in the west the long highland country extending from New Mexico to Chile; then at both ends of the continent, and more or less in all of its eastern section, notice the expanse of the lowland countries that comprehends the larger part of Canada, the United States in the north, and Argentine, Uruguay, and Brazil in the south.

p. 10

In order to get a more accurate picture let us imagine a provisional map of the continent: a three-color map corresponding to the threefold topographical sections of our continent, or rather to the three sections made by history out of the two large divisions of lowland and highland, the lowland being inhabited by the Anglo-Saxons in the north and by the Latins in the south. We could give color then to our map by spreading, say for instance, a golden yellow over all the section of the highland, considering its closeness to the sun—a few miles of altitude, of nearness to, or of distance from, the sun meaning so much in the schedules of our planet. After covering up with yellow all this highland section, we could take a green color to indicate the fertile plains of the Amazonas and the Pampas, and then we could put a vivid pink upon the surface of Northeastern America, from the edge of the California-Colorado plateau, way down to Florida and up to Quebec.

Before going any farther we shall remark that there is a complete similarity between the green and the pink areas, especially in the northern and southern ends. The Canada-United States northern section has practically the same sort of soil as the Argentine and Southern Brazil territory. Both are lowland countries of temperate climate, level

In other words, the technique of civilization will not have to change in going through the enormous surface of the lowland continent.

Between the northern and the southern sections there is only one factor of difference, a factor large enough though to justify the different shadow of color in our map. As everybody knows it is the Anglo-Saxon race that has created the astounding North American civilization that is changing the axis of history from the Old World to the New. On the other hand, we have in the south a curiously similar sociological situation, developing under identical natural circumstances, but with the Latin instead of the Anglo-Saxon stamina. If you consider, for instance, the rapid development of territories like San Paulo of Brazil, if you study the figures of town and factory increase, you immediately realize that the fabulous rise of the American Middle West is being matched both agriculturally and industrially by the Latin inhabitants of Brazil, who are working under conditions similar in nature to the North American conditions. Only the fact that the Latin spirit predominates in the lowland of the south while the Anglo-Saxon spirit rules the north will justify our provisional three-colored division of the map of our continent.

The threefold division is in reality only a provisional one, because we have in the center of the continent, or rather in the center of the lowlands of the continent, a huge area that falls in the realm of what we know as the tropical country-the hot country, practically unconquered yet by man. In fact the distinction is so important that the whole question of civilized life in the tropics should be undertaken as a separate problem. We shall have to point out in our map and consequently we shall have to imagine a fiery color spread over the surface of the whole section composed of the coast of Mexico and all of the Antilles, plus the coast of

p. 13

Central America, Venezuela, and all of the Amazonas territory.

Although we shall not go very deeply into the discussion of the tropical problem, let us say in passing that it is probably the most vital problem for the future of our whole continent-perhaps even the most vital for the future of humanity at large. The mere idea of the possibility of mastering the tropical climate and resources open before our imagination some thing like the eve of a new epoch in human culture.

A period is bound to come in which humanity, duly provided with a powerful technique, will undertake the conquest and exploitation of the torrid zone. With a highly developed equipment, man will start in the south a task similar to what was done thousands of years ago when, through the invention of fire and the use of combustion, the wild hunter succeeded in mastering and adapting for civilization the snow-covered plains of the cold and the temperate zones. The complete outline of our map will have to be redrawn; the whole process of history will change on the same day that we shall become capable of handling cold waves and chemical processes, and able to enter into the jungles of Brazil and Colombia and Mexico to impose there the rule of man and the purpose of man. But

p. 14

it would take us too far away if we stop to consider the possibilities of this sort of task. Let us only mention the fact of the existence of these four natural sections in our continent, insisting a little further upon the problem of the nations located in what we have called the highland as compared with the future of the nations living in the lowlands of the two ends of the New World and which constitute two undisputed successes—the colossal success of the United States and the Canadian Empire and the rapidly increasing success of the Brazil-Argentina Latin civilization.

First we shall try to compare the northern type of homogeneous culture with the southern type of homogeneous culture, and after that we will pass judgment on the heterogeneous, composite type of culture represented by the highland and the tropical nations.

It is easy to notice from the start, that the differences between Anglo-Saxon North America and Latin South America are only racial, temperamental, spiritual differences. The material problems of both civilizations are very similar if not the same. Both civilizations are governed by the normal law of associated kindred efforts that combine to build a great nation. None of the two lowland territories has suffered from the tragic rhythm of contrast

p. 15

which we have pointed out as being the rule of the highland type of civilization. This alone explains why the Latin and the Anglo-Saxon, although so different in temperament, have yet succeeded in creating, each one in its own area, that wonder of the New World-the possibility of populations and cities developing overnight. The truth is that there is nothing extraordinary in this sort of achievement. Take any moderately capable branch of the human tamily, give it tertile plains and rivers to carry its boats and its crops and you will be repeating the old-time story of every regular normal civilization. If to these favorable surroundings you add a certain number of good seaports, then sooner or later there will arise one of those outgrowing cultures that have created the fabric of modern life. There has never been any considerable doubt expressed as to the future of Argentina, just as nobody doubted fifty years ago that the Yankees were bound to create a strong modern nation. Where the problem was to enlarge and multiply the prairies of England and the herds of Scotland, all that was required was a sort of cumulative effort, one generation adding naturally to the work of the other generation and everybody contributing to the increase of the unlimited figures of a prosperity that could be calculated beforehand. It is

p. 16

very easy to collect results from a territory of such a sort. It is very easy to be a prophet where all the elements of a national growth are given as fixed normal elements. The same can be said of temperate Argentina. Humanity has tried once and again, and always with a fair measure of success, all of this cumulative process of civilization where nothing is left to chance or to fate. But the task changes in scope and in nature the moment you leave the trodden road to enter into the trackless unknown of uncontrollable natural elements. It is then, perhaps, not so much a question of race capacities as it is mainly the nature of the territory and the peculiar task of every given period of history that one should have in mind in order to judge fairly on a given situation. To make this point even more clear, allow me to give you an instance taken from the present condition of our own American continent.

It very often has been said that the Spanish system of colonization has proved to be a failure because none of the old Spanish-American colonies has been able to compete with the astounding progress of the United States. For some reason or other almost everybody accepts the belief that the Spanish system of colonization and culture cannot compare with its rival, the English system of

p. 17

colonization in America. But when you start to investigate the reasons of such supposed failure, immediately not one but a hundred facts and circumstances spring up to demonstrate that the Spanish encountered and solved difficulties such as never appeared before the English invader. We shall not undertake to consider this historical question, but judging the problem even by our present standards there is a very significant question that I have once before submitted to a learned audience. The question is the following: Why is it that the English, the Dutch, and the French, three firstclass races, have had such slight success in their sleepy old colonies of the three Guianas? What have these three powerful races done in their South American lot? They have done considerably less than what the Spaniards did in the similar surrounding territories of Venezuela and Yucatan and the coast of Mexico. Why is it then that the three all-gifted nations of Northern Europe have failed in the same territories where Spain was able to achieve a considerable amount of success? Does this instance not show at least that perhaps we have been too quick to underestimate the civilizing effort of the Spaniard in tropical America, instead of realizing that our civilization as a whole is not yet prepared to meet the obstacles of heat and big

p. 18

rivers and unfavorable elements? And notice further that the Guianas are a virgin country while Mexico and the rest of the highland country is not a virgin territory, not a barren, empty zone, but a land more complicated precisely because it is filled with residues of past civilizations, haunted with phantasms of noble failures that call to our will for a new opportunity of development.

Again coming back to the lowland type of civilization represented by the Anglo-Saxon and the Latin nations at opposite ends of the continent, let us say also that, although both have in common the temperate climate and the consequent homogeneous type of civilization, there is still between them a difference in spiritual nature which is bound to give different, though in each case very admirable, results.

It is hard to define any social subject with any amount of fairness because every definition implies a more or less arbitrary selection of certain features at the expense and to the exclusion of others. It would take volumes, simply to try to point out what we mean when we speak of the Latin temperament transplanted to America and what we mean by the Anglo-Saxon of the New World. But taking for granted that our statements will have to be incomplete and sometimes overdrawn, we are bound

p. 19

to repeat that there exists a deep, important difference between the people of Latin America and the people of Anglo-Saxon America. The difference is so much more important because it is a difference of a spiritual sort. Perhaps it lies in the manner of feeling and in the manner of expressing our feelings of life. It is in the type of our higher desires and in the manner we go about our pleasures that we can best be distinguished one from the other. The physiological, the elementary material needs of our nature are practically the same in all the human family; we all need food and shelter, but the moment the practical need is quieted through satisfaction or control, and as soon as we conquer leisure, a series of different longings and desires will arise. What is a pleasure to me, may not be a pleasure to others; and such things as delight a certain kind of people may displease or embarrass another.

In the manner of enjoying life-there is where the thinker should search; where the artist should discover those traits that enable him to portray the soul of a nation or a period. And when one speaks of pleasure, one naturally has to take up the literature, the art, the emotion, the soul-lifting expres sions of a country and its epoch. In the manner of enjoying life and in the manner of expressing the

p. 20

higher purposes of life the Latin and the Anglo-Saxon of America are different, just as the two parental races are different in the old continent. And it is only natural that this should be so, because we are a sort of readaptation of the Mediterranean standards and manners of life while the North Americans are a sort of enlarged Baltic civilization. And the fact that European civilization is a solidly inspired forward movement of humanity, notwithstanding the internal differences of Europe-indeed exactly on account of those internal differences—should teach us in America that it is possible to build a strong powerful civilization of the New World without trying to impose one type of civilization upon the other, without trying even to argue which is the better, whether the Latin or the Anglo-Saxon; for both are useful, and perhaps both are indispensable for the present and the future power and glory of the continent. It is not by suppressing differences that progress opens its way; it is through differences—deeply inspired, sincere differences-that the human soul has made all of its conquests. And the explanation is a very simple one: it is only material differences that create bitterness and rivalry among men. Spiritual differences on the contrary tend to the tightening of human bonds, because we find pleasure in every

p. 21

difference of the taste; and every peculiarity enriches the wealth of the species. In itself spiritual wealth is such that everybody can enjoy it without using it up, but on the contrary the more you make use of it the richer in value it becomes.

The fact that this continent is divided among Anglo-Saxons and Latins should be looked upon then as a blessing; because we all long for a higher, richer spiritual world, and it is only through the work of singularly gifted groups of people that a true all-comprehending type of civilization may come to life. The bigoted patriot who may dream of an all-Yankee America extending its shops from Patagonia to Canada is just as potent a foe to the true ends of mankind as the blind Latin-American chauvinist who may dream of a time of decay in which the Yankee standards of life are to be substituted by the newer southern manners of life. The one comforting fact is that differences of temperament and of soul tend to create sympathy and friendliness rather than opposition and scorn, and this should be borne in mind whenever any conflict of a material character may arise among our nations. The other evident fact is the oneness of the temperamental type of the people living south of the Tropic of Cancer.

In the south we are one, morally and racially,

p. 22

and this unity is not destroyed through our threefold geographic, ethnographic map, just as the Colorado Mountains do not break the Yankee spirit of the northern country. Fortunately some peculiarities and modes of expression of the soul do not follow slavishly the boundaries and varieties of the soil. The highland has its own problems, and the tropic has its own problems; but above this a similar cultural tradition maintains a perfect unity between the people of the Antilles and those of the Mexican Plateau, who constitute an almost identical type with the inhabitants of Argentina, Chile, or Peru. In other words we might again present the problem by saying that the Latin culture is represented in America by the three geographical working zones we have been defining: the peculiar highland, the tropical land, and the southern lowland inhabited by Latins. If we were to draft a soul-map of the continent instead of the working map we have been mentioning, then we should have to use just one color from the Rio Grande to the Tierra del Fuego, including the Antilles, and another color for the United States and Canada; and thus we would have before us the two forms of civilization, the two manners of interpreting life, that we must try to combine in a brotherly manner if our purpose is as it should be, to create in this

p. 23

hemisphere a new cycle of history-the cycle of liberty, understanding, and love amongst all races and nations.

But again, as soon as we start to consider what Latin America really is we fall fatally into the three subdivisions already outlined—subdivisions that very plainly indicate to us the threefold task of our culture. Thus we have, as a map suited to working purposes, the four distinctive zones, the four cultural zones of the New World: (I) the North American, Anglo-Saxon zone of the United States and Canadian lowlands; (2) the highland or Sierra Madre, Andean Plateau of temperate climate and mixed Indian-Spanish populations; (3) the torrid, or tropical, zone consisting of Cuba, Santo Domingo, and Puerto Rico, plus the coast of Mexico and Central America and the hot low countries of Venezuela and Colombia, with the immense unexplored zone of the Amazonas and the Orinoco; (4) and farther south, the Latin lowlands of Argentina and Brazil.

I have called this fourfold subdivision a working subdivision, and neither the name nor the classifications are arbitrary; on the contrary they both correspond exactly to living undeniable realities. We all know that the richest, vastest, most promising soil of the New World lies in the Torrid Zone,

p. 24

a place where nobody has yet been able to live and to prosper. This presents a task that will come into execution in some future time, perhaps close to our day, perhaps still distant; but let us have in mind that the race that shall conquer the tropics will be the master of the future. In regard to Latin-American problems we must consider that one of Latin-America's greatest handicaps has been this inheritance of the tropical zone-an inheritance that may develop tomorrow into untold wonders, but that today stands as a weight on our shoulders and as a barrier to our effort.

It is time now to consider the problem of the highland as it appears, for instance, in Mexico. We find that Mexico, as we have already pointed out, is a mixture of climates, of races, and cultures; but still more perplexing it happens that Mexico is the zone where nature has put, one opposing the other, the two most powerful but least controllable elements: the tropical heat and the mountain. From whatever way you approach the problem of civilization in Mexico you will find the strength of these two formidable barriers of human enterprise: excessive heat in certain sections, impassable mountains in other places. We have already stated that the tropical heat may be converted in some near future into an advantage instead of a handicap.

p. 25

Can we say the same of the mountain? I believe we can; but, of course, not under the present almost elementary condition of human technique.

Mexico is one of the most mountainous countries in the world; wherever you go, excepting only the northern end of the plateau, you encounter the sierra; you meet with the mountain in all its grandeur and its charm, with all its majesty and its might. Hard indeed it seems to establish any degree of civilized standards in the midst of a nature that seems adapted for the bird rather than for man. Building roads in such a territory, as well as tunnels, means such a terrific effort that the whole system of man's labor seems checked and postponed the very moment that you meet with the masses of the Cordillera. The best proof that we have not entirely overcome, even with our minds, all of this awe and power of the mountain, can be found in the emotion with which we read today historical accounts of the crossing of the Alps by Hannibal, or the crossing of the Andes by Bolivar's armies.

If the occasional feat of transporting through the mountains is still considered an admirable enterprise, imagine what it means to be forced to establish a regular road-system through the ravine and the summit. Imagine what it means to be

If we consider then that Mexico, like Peru and Bolivia and Colombia, is a sort of meeting place of all of the most powerful and yet uncontrolled elements of nature the sierra and the heat, the flood and the earthquake-and if we add to these natural handicaps to the ways of man the other conflicting factors of race and history, we shall readily understand the reason for the continued and colossal failures of civilized effort in our country. And after reflecting upon these circumstances, will there be anybody bold enough to condemn a race or a series of races that have been struggling under such exceptionally difficult conditions? Are we not, on the other hand, authorized to state that we are working under the pressure of a series of obstacles and hardships such as no other people have ever encountered?

At least we must seek in the existence of all of these handicaps the reason for many of our national traits, both the bad and the good traits. Take, for instance, our individual, our national psychology. We have been raised in the mountains; we have been living out of the mountains—that is to say, we have been depending on mining for our living and our prosperity. From Potosí to Zacatecas and

p. 27

Guanajuato the highland of America, together with the northern end of California, has been supplying the world with silver and gold. Our forefathers, after completing the conquest, devoted themselves to the extraction of the ore from the vein. They did this under the same risky, bold circumstances with which they had carried on the conquest of the land. Mining at that time was an adventure as doubtful as the expeditions and combats for the discovery of the Eldorados. Conquerors, discoverers, explorers of the unknown surfaces of the continent, and later miners who explored the unknown underground in search of Aladdin's wealth-all of them were adventurous heroes with' uncertain tasks to fulfil. It is only natural that such ways of living should have affected the feeling of the race toward life. People that get into the habit of adventure necessarily develop traits of imagination and occasional daring followed by long periods of relaxation—traits such as are necessary for their particular form of living. In some respects the national character becomes spoiled; we become accustomed to await everything ; from the unexpected. Our psychology as a rule is the psychology of the gambler; we gamble with our wealth and with our chances in life; in fact, we even gamble with our lives. A popular saying is often applied

p. 28

whenever we hesitate before unknown, possibly unexpected, developments: A ver que sale, "Let us see what comes out of it." By it we mean not fatalism but a sort of a lottery sentiment, a decision to try fate and to force it to give out its secret and its treasure. The A ver que sale really means: "Let us put faith in the outcome; after we have done our best let the uncontrollable do its caprice." Such is our normal attitude toward life; such was the attitude of the explorer of the wilderness and the attitude of the miner of the latter day. Such is the attitude imposed on man by the mountain and by all uncontrolled forces of nature. A similar psychology might be traced perhaps even in the Yankee miner of the first epoch because nature imposed its influence upon man in this wild mysterious highland of the New World. There is a sort of sentiment which one might call the bonanza sentiment, which pervades our soul with all the strength of enthusiasm but also with the deteriorating effect of dismay of the present and doubt about the future.

All the effects of the mountain upon man are far-reaching. The civilizations of the plains go along increasing through centuries in an added enjoyment of wealth that is only interrupted by war and jealousy of less fortunate neighbors. But a

Even the city that the miner builds is a temporary city. Placed as a rule on the slopes of the hills that surround the mining camp, the city has to bring from afar and has to pay dearly for its food and its water. It does not matter when the times are prosperous, but a collapse becomes inevitable the very moment the vein is exhausted. And after the vein is exhausted no attachment can be maintained with the soil, the mining area as a rule being unfit for cultivation. There is then nothing left to do but to emigrate, or at least to come down to the more hospitable valley, there to start anew, undergoing poverty after the days of lavish expenditure and splendor. Even the only remaining testimony of good times is condemned to become a ruin. I refer to the gorgeous churches, to the handsome palaces that fill such old towns as Guanajuato of Mexico and Quito of Ecuador, so many marks of the height of the bonanza wave. Left alone it would have probably fallen into the curse of the highland, into the irregular, perplexing rhythm of the brightness and shadow of ardent hopes and painful despair-the law of contrasts that constitutes the rule of life in the plateau.

What is, then, the conclusion that we should draw from such circumstances? Should we state that the soil of Mexico and the soil of the highland are unfit for a true civilization, for a powerful civilization, and are consequently bound to the tragedy of flashing prospects and then irrevocable decay?

How could we account then for the fact that Mexico and Peru, during the seventeenth and the early eighteenth centuries, were the centers of the only civilization then existing on the continent - a civilization that created an architectural

p. 31

style like the Mexican churriguera that today is giving inspiration to the most important development of the present-day North American architecture; a civilization that printed the first books and issued the first newspapers of the continent; that built roads through the desert extending from the Mexican Plateau to the wild immense plains of Arizona and New Mexico; a civilization and a national wealth that had the power to overflow its territory in order to contribute to the building of forts and palaces in such distant places as the island of Puerto Rico and the missions of California? How could such an effort come to naught by break and fall? And, first of all, how did it happen to achieve what it did achieve?

All of these perplexing questions are easily understood by those who have been in Mexico, by those who have felt the fascination of the highland power. The explanation lies in that same law of contrast and irregularity, in that rhythm of the unexpected possibilities and calamities-the law to which I have been referring as peculiar to the civilization of the highland.

It is not only the bonanza that intervenes to create among us periods of renascence and forescence and periods of decay; it is all of the factors of nature combined, that lead in Mexico to this con-

stant uncertainty and to the corresponding periodical offspring of the higher capacities of life. The mountain and the climate constitute a handicap to steady, regulated progress, but the mountain and the climate also constitute a treasure. The highland succeeded when it devoted itself to its proper task, the task of mining; but when it was found necessary through the exhaustion of the mine or through changed conditions in the world-market to change from the mine industry to the agricultural or the manufacturing industry, it was found that we lacked in the highland most of the essential equipment. We had no irrigation works to protect our planted fields from the irregularities or the scarcity of rainfall; we had no combustible, no coal, to generate the steam that moved factories. For the moment-and in the life of nations centuries are like mere moments-for a moment we found ourselves in the midst of a world that had changed its tools without giving us notice. Absorbed as we were, and even infatuated with our wealth in silver and gold, we did not realize that an era was developing in which coal and steel would become more valuable than gold and would succeed in putting to its service all the gold of our mines and of the world's mines. And perhaps the main reason for the evident decay we have shown in this last cen-

Our mining industry in such a situation became nothing but a luxury; and it is always the powerful, the vitally powerful, that makes the terms for the value of any luxury, Since then we have not found our way economically. It takes time and it takes suffering to change the mode of living of any people, and it is always harder to imitate than to invent. Even the ability for inventing goes along with economic demand and success. At the time of the supreme importance of the precious-metal industry, we had in Mexico the most advanced technical methods of the time. The best methods for assaying and smelting ores were local Mexican inventions. In later days how could we be expected to invent in mechanics, if we hardly knew the modern machines except through pictures in books and magazines? It is not then so much a question of race ability as a ques tion of the world-importance of the task that every nation is undertaking in a certain given time. In the period of the adaptation of motor power to human industry we have had to be spectators. But the question now is: What is this new tool in the hands of man going to mean for us? May it not

possibly happen that such tools, though not originated in our territory, are still apt to render a large profit in our lands, larger even than in the place of their origin? It is true that our case might have appeared to be hopeless when machinery was moved only by steam power. We had neither the stuff out of which a mechanical civilization is made nor the necessary useful combustible. But the very instant that modern industry began to utilize electrical power instead of steam power, the whole aspect of the future changed to our benefit. Because what other country in the world can rival Mexico in the lavish, immeasurable amounts of utilizable energy that today goes to waste in the annual, natural rhythm of the fogs that rise from the sea in order to make the clouds, and the wind that takes the clouds over the mountain tops in order to cover the plateau with a rainfall that comes rushing down, every season, through the narrow passes of the ravines and through the saltos of the thousand grades of levels? And every one of these changes in level is like an inexhaustible coal mine— a perpetual source of energy, that will allow us to build, some day, one of the most potent manufacturing centers of history. So in this way, in our land of mystery and contrast, the mountain that has been a handicap is apt to become, tomorrow,

Plans for the industrial electrification of countries are now discussed everywhere, but there is probably no other section of the world that is more fit for this sort of advancement than Mexico; and notice, if you will, that this advantage comes to us precisely in the combinations of the two disadvantages that appeared to be fatal-the heat and the mountain. For if it were not for the heat, the clouds could not carry mountains of water to be dropped over the mountain of rock, producing thus the electrical current that is there waiting for the mastery of man.

It is enough to consider the unlimited possibilities of a development of the sort just outlined in order to understand that Mexico is not a hopeless backward country, but a wonderful promise—more than that, a certainty that can not be delayed very much longer. The hour of Mexico has not yet sounded, but it is there already marked in the clock of progress. The hour of the whole of the highland will be a wonderful hour. So long as electricity remains the governing power of human industry, the highland will be able to retain the leadership among nations.

There are many other elements that contribute to mark the differences between the highland and the lowland, between Mexico and, for instance, Argentina and Eastern United States. The most important of these elements after the physical characteristics already discussed is the racial element. The racial element of the highland is different from the racial element of the lowland. We have already referred to the fact that the United States is racially a mixture of Northern European bloods while Argentina and Southern Brazil are composed of Southern European stock. The highland is more complex on account of the Indians, or rather on account of the different races of Indians that inhabit it; and also on account of the mestizo, who as everybody knows came into existence through the mixture of the Spanish and the Indian bloods.

But whenever one compares sincerely the mental aptitude or the moral qualities of the Indian with the other races of the world one is convinced that the Indian is the possessor of as many good qualities as any other living race. You will easily realize that, if you apply truly superior standards of judgment. I know the extent of race prejudice, but is it not the duty of the mind to study dispassionately and draw its conclusions in accordance with a sense of the higher meaning of life? And is not the race question, however you approach it, one in which almost any statement receives credence? A thing that happens only because we know little about it!

Observe, though, the handicraft of the Indian; observe it in the architecture of the prehistoric times; and watch it at work in the present-day small industry of the mountain-section villages. There is not one side of the question in which different views cannot be advanced. Take, for instance, a question apparently trivial—the question of good looks. The main reason why most people retain their race prejudices is because a certain aesthetical instinct leads us to search for beauty in our fellow-beings. The white race, the predominant race of today, often finds that the other racial types are ugly. The other racial types may not always find that the white man is a sort of god descended from the sun; but still, as a rule, they allow themselves to be fascinated by the white man's standards of beauty. You find almost in every one of our Indian or mestizo poets, dark of skin themselves, the ardent eulogy of the white hands and the pale cheeks of the amada. In other words, a race of slaves cannot be beautiful because hard work and misery tend to leave their imprint on the body. There is not in all Europe a prettier stock than that Celtic Portuguese race that produces some of the best flavored wines of the world. Leisure and wealth develop beauty in any racial stock.

The racial problems we are mentioning in a passing way are accentuated in the highland but are not entirely peculiar to the highland. Argentina and Brazil reach and take into their bosom extensive provinces like Tucuman and El Chaco or Parana and Misiones in which racial conditions are very similar to the Mexican or the Bolivian conditions. It would then be absurd to think that the purely Latin European people of the Argentine coast were going to constitute a caste apart from the mestizo and the Indian of the interior. Such a thing would be as ignoble as to become Argentinian in order to persist as a porteño—a citizen of Buenos Aires. The process there, just as in the rest of Latin America, is of amalgamation with the native stock. The descendants of the Italian and the Pole in one or two generations become criollos, and thus fall into the common type of the continental Latin-American civilization. In this way, gradually, and notwithstanding nature's differences in soil and climate, we are becoming more and more identical. We are bound together by a common past, a common tongue, a common culture, and even our institutions and our problems are the same from the moral and spiritual point of view.

The time is nearing when the tropic will be mastered and when the intricacies of the highland will be converted into usefulness. We have a world-mission to fulfil. The task involves such a tremendous effort that we do not pretend that we are going to develop, alone, all this new era of history. We know that today more than ever the work of civilization requires the concerted effort, the collaboration of every human being no matter from whence he comes. Our standard then should be a free open standard-free not only from the point of view of public rights but also free from prejudice, free through its wide power of brotherly emotion—a free heart, strong, and always ready for all sorts of love.

DEMOCRACY IN LATIN AMERICA

A vital question in Latin America, and one that is often debated is this: Should all the Latin-American states remain loyal to their written constitutions dating from the beginnings of the nineteenth century that establish the sovereignty of the people expressed by the vote, or is it better to look for more native standards such as military dictatorships in the name of the republic or in the name of socialism? Let us say from the start that the question is only debated in such places as have to suffer from the military dictators and from those people that wish to please the military dictators. It would not be fair to state that Argentina or Brazil have any sort of sympathy for military rule; both of them are too civilized to compare even in theory the imperfect rule of democracy—imperfect though as it is——with the troglodite methods of our military dictators. But if Argentina and Brazil and Colombia and Cuba and Costa Rica succeeded in getting rid, fifty years ago, of personal leadership and dictatorship of the type we call "caudillism" — the rule of the military—there is another large group of na-

p. 44

tions where local petty caesarism is constantly reasserting itself and maintaining hold, through ignorance, through cowardice, through lies, and through murder and theft.

And as the question of passing judgment on such methods of government, when applied to our lands, is not only discussed by local talent but also very often advocated by enlightened foreigners, I wish to take advantage of the hospitality of this University of Chicago—a hospitality generously granted to truth and the good cause of human understanding—in order to present a few suggestions to contradict the opinion of those that are constantly talking of the iron hand and the strong oneman rule that is supposed to achieve success in our more backward Latin-American countries.

The struggle between democratic methods and dictatorial methods of government is not a new one in our continent. The Indians of pre-Cortesian times were slaves to god-like dictators, that governed the state in a manner, very similar to the manner of modern military caudillos. The monarch of old represented the state; the caudillo of the times of Santa Anna represented liberalism or centralism; and the caudillo and dictator of today represents order, for instance, in Venezuela, or radicalism in Mexico. At bottom they all represent the

p. 45

one-man savage rule of the Oriental type of despotisms of yesterday and of today. In fact, there is very little difference between a Sikh pascha of Asia and a soldier-president of Latin-American history -the difference consisting in that the people of Latin America have never tolerated a long dictatorship and have always succeeded in punishing the dictator, through liberating triumphant revolutions. But revolution again brings into power the victorious revolutionist chieftain, and again we fall into the old ways and the same abuses; and so democracy, that in many cases has not even been tried, is scolded and ridiculed, only to serve better the shameful interests of one-man rule.

Such has been the development of events, but the one thing we should try to rescue from all this tragedy is the validity and the usefulness of a certain number of principles without which no nation can survive; and among these principles the democratic form of government is one of the most essential.

One of the puzzles of old Mexican history and of old Peruvian history is the facility with which a small group of adventurers succeeded in overturning and subduing the all-powerful empires of the Aztecs and the Incas. And yet if we give the matter a moment's attention we shall find the simple les-

p. 46

son that history once and again repeats for us in its pages—the lesson which says that a despotism can never survive in a clash with a democracy. All of you remember the classical story of Sparta and Athens defeating the huge armies of despotism under Darius, the incarnate state and God of the Persians. A free parliament appointing generals that commanded free men; citizens of small republics, but subject to no man-these were enough to destroy the most powerful empire of the time.

The Spaniards that came to conquer America were a wonderful sort of men, brigands if you like, but free brigands, independent and haughty in their own land. So when they arrived in Mexico and observed the poor Indian, trembling before the mere name of his emperor, the all-powerful Monteexperienced a gay temptazuma, they must have tion to show the poor Indian what the god-like Montezuma was really worth. And they did so, and as soon as Montezuma was captured, the whole empire crumbled, because one-man countries and one-man situations follow the fate of the idol and pay for the sin of man-worship. The Spanish conquerors were the remnants of the army that fought the Arabs under Isabella, and Isabella was the chief of a group of more or less independent kingdoms and towns that had their own fuero, their own mu-

nicipal liberties. At the time of Isabella it was yet true that every Spaniard felt he was as good as the king, and any two of them, better than the king, just as the famous saying puts it. So it is evident that the clash between the Indian and the Spaniard in the days of Cortes and Pizarro was a clash between the democracy of the time-one of the most genuine democracies of Europeand despotism— one of the crudest despotisms of history, the despotism of Montezuma and the Inca. I doubt very much that the Spaniards of the time of Philip II would have been able to repeat the enterprise of conquering a world. When a nation is afraid of its own government, very little can be done against a foreign enemy or in a foreign enterprise.

And if the feat of these bands of freemen conquering kingdoms of enslaved people is amazing, the way they organized things after the conquest is not less admirable. Every new settlement and every old town was always organized, not in accordance with the indigenous habit of dependence to a certain chief, but in the good Spanish way of organizing a city council. The free and ardent proselytism of the missionary was another civilizing and democratic influence. And it was only in the later days, in the days of decay, in the days of emperors that had made no conquests, emperors by chance like Charles V and monsters like Philip I, it was only then that the king's envoy, and the judge of the inquisition, began to degrade and corrupt the wonderful original organization.

Still, the undoubted fruition of Spanish culture in the America of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries was due to the comparatively democratic methods of government and to the fair-minded and liberal policies of such viceroys as Hurtado de Mendoza and the Revillagigedos, who were men with the modern concept and vision of government, according to whom government is a service and a duty toward the community and not a privilege and a joy of the despot.

But with Spanish decay and with Spanish despotism at home, the colonies gradually came down to the same low political standard: The King and the Church-the King represented by the soldier, and the Church represented by the Inquisition. Both, closely allied, became supreme and governed unopposed to the misfortune of the people.

The present-day Revolution is no exception, as the situation in Mexico to a large extent remains in the hands of people who in many cases antagonized Madero, or at least did not take any part in Madero's uprising. Many of them came into the Revolution, only after Madero had demonstrated that it was a comparatively easy job to overthrow despots; and it is only natural that such people who came to the Revolution, only after it was proved that revolutions were good business, should be trying to imitate Porfirio Diaz's methods of one-man rule rather than Madero's democratic doctrines.

But the irrepressible self-asserting revolutionary spirit of the Latin-American nations is enough to show that we shall have no enduring peace on this continent so long as there are one-man governments and dictatorships in our nations. The strug-

gle of the Latin-American revolutionist is the struggle of democratic European ideas to impose themselves upon the Oriental indigenous type of despotism and upon the decayed soldier-rule of the days of Ferdinand the Coward. All the petty Napoleons of our history, all the Caesars that "have never known defeat," are nothing but incubators of revolutions; and so long as they exist peace cannot exist, because the Latin-American nations are pledged in soul to the democratic ideal of liberty and equality, which is the only basis of any truly civilized form of social arrangement.

The first man who really understood the problem of our social development was the Argentine statesman and writer Sarmiento. His views were very clearly and dramatically exposed in a book that should be read carefully by any person who wishes to understand the Latin-American sociology. The book is a novel called Facundo; the hero of the novel, Facundo, is the embodiment of that type of leader that comes when the revolution is over, claiming to be the revolution but only putting in practice, for his own benefit, the same abusive methods of the past. Facundo is the military chieftain who imposes his will through terrorism and does away with the law and with the sense of human sympathy. The only policy of Facundo is the

p. 53

blood-stained policy of the firing squad: whoever is not with him is against him, and whoever is against him should be shot as enemy of the country or as an enemy of the revolution. With the times, the name of the program changes; but the method does not change. In the time of Facundo the enemies of Facundo were classed as federalistas—defenders of the Argentine federation, and thus were shot. In latter days the enemies of a despot are called enemies of public order and shot, such was the case of the Diaz régimes; in later days the enemies of the military dictator are classified as "reactionaries" and are poisoned or shot; but the system is the same —the system is despotism in one of its more brutal and degraded forms.

Sarmiento devoted all his life to eradicate this militarism, this caudillism, from the Argentine soil. And after a long, noble struggle Sarmiento succeeded in gaining control, first, of the educational movement of his country, later, of full power by becoming the president of Argentina. Then Sarmiento, as president, managed to carry along the policy outlined in his novel years before: the policy of Europeanization of Latin America; the policy of democratization of Latin America, as against the policy of the caudillos that are constantly struggling for personal rule. In the ancestral struggle

p. 54

between democracy and dictatorship, the government of Sarmiento is the first notable success of our continent, and from the success of Sarmiento is derived the present prosperity and greatness of the greatest of the Spanish-American nations, our sister-republic of Argentina. Had the caudillos won, over the intellectual Sarmiento, the situation of Argentina would not be today any better than that of Bolivia; but after the enlightened rule of Sarmiento, the reappearance of the ignorant caudillo was made impossible.

The unquestionable success of Argentina has very often been attributed to its temperate climate, to European immigration, to the scarcity of Indian population, etc.; but I believe that the main factor of Argentine's progress is to be found in the victory that Argentina was able to win over the military chieftain, over the caudillo that has ravaged the rest of Spanish America. And my reasons for supporting this opinion are as follows: Argentina herself was unable to take advantage of its temperate climate, wealth, etc., during the long sterile years of the Rosas dictatorship. And it was only when the democratic revolution overthrew the dictatorship that Argentina started to become a nation. Then, if we take in another section of the continent another case of success, perhaps the only case of

You do not find generals in the annals of Costa Rican history, but since the early. times you find, over there, fairly good schools and a general desire for progress and for civilized human manners of living.

Jumping again to the south we find that Chile, a nation that has had no caudillos, no military chieftains in power, has at the same time maintained a very fair standard of progress and a fairly civilized system of government, although in recent years the weight of the reigning oligarchy upon the working classes has become pitifully dangerous. And of course with oppression and backwardness military dictatorship is there, at present, a latent when not an actual calamity. The origin of Chile's greatness lies in the statesmanship of men of the type of Bello, an educator, a grammarian, a man of letters, in power.

The only case in which an intellectual group actually came into power in Mexico, in all the history of Mexico, is, at the same time, the only decent period of the history of Mexico and the only progressive period. In fact, it was the time when Mexico was able to write pages into the history of the world; the drama of old-time autocracy and militarism represented by Maximilian, struggling against democracy and right represented by Juarez, has become one of the subjects of the world's literature. The only real progress ever made in Mexico comes also from the time of democracy initiated by Juarez and continued by Lerdo. It was then (remember, this was in the sixties) that the first and the best railroad of the country was built, the first railroads, the first public schools; and, together with material advantages, a complete reform of the social order—a democratic, liberal reform—the organization of public life outside the bonds of the

It has always been noted that the generation of politicians and statesmen that accompanied Juarez in his task after years of public services did not accumulate wealth and never produced the type of the revolutionist hacendado and financier. They did produce splendid financiers like Dublan and Matias Romero, men who reorganized the fiscal systems of the country and brought money into the national treasury after covering all public needs; but they never produced any financier such as military governments develop —the financier who dismisses clerks because he cannot pay the government services and closes schools because it is necessary to economize, but who himself is able to emerge from the absolute destitution of his prepolitical obscurity to the position of millionaire and nouveau riche of an impoverished nation.

Most people believe, particularly outside of Mexico, that the most progressive period of our history was due to the iron rule of Porfirio Diaz. I do not deny some of the strong qualities of Porfirio Diaz, particularly after one observes his imitators succeeding by copying all of his vices and none

But he would not have been able to retain power for such a length of time if he had suffered from that base passion and greed of his later disciples.

Admitting then that Porfirio Diaz was probably the best possible type of a despot, yet we shall have to condemn him and his system the moment we stop to think that any despotism of a personal character is limited to the period of life of the despot. And the despot leaving no political inheritors— such as only monarchs can leave —is always unable to take care of the future. So I feel entitled to say to all those defenders of the iron rule, to all the believers in personal rule and caudillism: If you do not want to see another cycle of revolution and chaos, such as the one we are now passing through in Mexico, do not lend any assistance to the personal aggrandizement of any Porfirio Diaz whether spurious or authentic, because every Porfirio Diaz is bound to bring behind him a new era of hate and destruction and chaos.

The fact that so many of the Latin nations are falling today into the hands of dictators constitutes only a sad sign of decay. One of the most dangerous factors that we have to deal with is the hypocrisy with which we make use of certain names. The case is beginning to be so frequent in our America, that it is necessary that we should define what is to be understood by democratic government and what is meant by autocratic, militaristic, dictatorial government.

It has become, in modern times, a sort of a fashion to criticize democracy, especially for the service of personal rule. The defects of democracy are carefully pointed out by the salaried secondrate intellectuals that put themselves at the service of the caudillo, and then the consequence is drawn that there is no use in fighting for democracy and that democracy is a failure. Let us repeat that democracy has proved to be defective, especially because it has found itself helpless in the hands of overambitious capitalism. But, can you compare the results of this defective democracy with the results of the Oriental systems of government, with the personal systems of government. As a rule it has always been the most democratic form of government that has created the larger, the more longlived empires of history. And also, naturally, it is only under democracy that the average man's standard of living is raised. Despotism on the contrary, as well as caudillism, tends to perpetuate the caste system. Every new caudillo carries behind him a family clan that robs and sacks the country in order to constitute the new aristocracy, the new godlike caste, the fonctionnaire caste of the period.

Let us insist that even if we wish to correct the vices of democracy, we must first of all have democracy, for it is only through democracy that we can achieve social progress. It is only through democracy that the constructive socialism of modern times has been made possible. And through dictatorship you obtain either the Latin-American caudillism or Russian bolshevism, that is to say, the caste system—the military caste of the backward countries of our America and the bureaucratic caste of bolshevist Russia. All of the political problems of Latin America, and very particularly the political problem of Mexico, can be summed up in these two words: "democracy" or "dictatorship." You will always find among the semi-intellectuals that live on the government, many that in every time of crisis will say, as all slaves always have said: Let us sacrifice all government principles, let us give up the constitution in order to save the nation." THE RACE PROBLEM IN LATIN AMERICA

Many theories have been advanced as to the origin of the old inhabitants of this New World. The discoveries of Professor Hrdlička, relating to strong similarities between a certain Siberian tribe and the North American Red Indian may be entirely correct, but they do not exclude the possibility of the existence of some more autocthonous stock coming from the very far south, from Patagonia, as the Peruvian legends seem to indicate. Certain similarities that have often been pointed out between the Maya Quiché architecture and the Egyptian manner of building, would tend to confirm the opinion of the believers in the Atlantis of the classic days; and finally, the theory of Professor Wegener regarding the original unity of all continents in one body which has been separated as a result of the earth's rotation would clear away all sorts of difficulties, for it would allow us to state that there is no more mystery attached to the aboriginal American than the mystery that surrounds the whole of the human race. Farther back than five thousand years we do not know how we lived, nor where.

p. 76

We can, however, assert that in the New World, just the same as in the Old, a series of civilizations have developed and decayed. Civilizations come and go, but it is very probable that the human stock, the particular race that creates a certain period of cultures, does not disappear with the disappearance of its constructive power. The social development of a certain people may stop entirely while the race itself goes on reproducing its members. The Greeks of the Roman days had disappeared as a ruling type of civilization but continued to exist as a race and traveled and mixed with other races; and the original stock thus became modified but never totally destroyed. The same thing can be said, I am sure, of the Indian. When Cortes arrived on the continent the Indians of Montezuma did not know themselves who had been the builders of the Teotihuacan pyramids that were lying covered with the dust of centuries a few miles away from the old Tenochtitlan—the Mexico City of today. But there is no doubt that a great number of these Montezuma Indians had in their veins the same blood of the builders of the forgotten monuments. The Aztecs, who were comparatively newcomers, may not have had it, but the strata of the older races inhabiting the valley, were no doubt inheritors of the flesh and the soul of the high-type

p. 77

ancestors. The invader never succeeded in destroying all of the subjected population, but had to coexist with them and naturally intermarried and learned from the older inhabitants. In the same manner we can affirm that, although the natives of Yucatan and Guatemala had forgotten all about the history of the builders of the Maya Quiché palaces, they still have in them the soul of the ancient architects. It is absolutely no wonder that a historical tradition should get lost in a time in which there was no written language; and even such written characters as existed were transmitted only through verbal tradition, with consequent danger of a total loss of the key to the symbols through periods of war, emigrations, and calamities.

The fact I am trying to emphasize then is that our race, the Indian races of the tropics at least, do not constitute a primitive stock. Call it, if you will, a decayed stock, but not a primitive stock. The new Mexico Indian may be primitive, although I would rather say that instead of being primitive he is provincial; that is to say, that what remains of his culture is derived from the little he could obtain, placed at such a distance from the higher civilizations of the southern sections of Guatemala and Mexico. I maintain that the rhythm of civil-

ization at that time, as well as in the time of the Spaniards and way up into the seventeenth century, the rhythm of civilization in the New World was, and had been, from south to north instead of being as it is today and as it has been since the Declaration of American Independence, from north to south. Since the beginning of the nineteenth century, the Latin-American countries began to take from the United States their political institutions, their school systems; and later we have taken the railroads, the machinery, and finally the capital for the development of our resources; but in the preceding centuries, during the day of the Indian and during the day of the Spaniard, it was the native of Arizona who had to march down to the south in search of tools for his wool-weaving and in search of inspiration for the drawings that decorate it. In the time of the Spaniard, the mission and the tillage both came from the better architecture and from the more advanced agriculture of Central Mexico, then the mother of Latin-American culture. In South America we find a similar, though inverted, movement. Civilization there centered about the semitropical and about the temperate plateau of Cuzco and scarcely reached the deserted plains of what is today the great growing Argentine nation. But the Aztec and the Inca sections were

Our Indians then are not primitive as was the Red Indian, but old, century-tried souls who have - known victory and defeat, life and death, and all of the moods of history.

I wish to call your attention to the fact that the Indians of Mexico as well as the Indians of Peru represented a certain type of civilization and consequently were not as the North American Indian simply tribes of natives, wandering tribes of hunters, because this in itself perhaps explains why the Spaniard had to mix with the Indian, while the Englishman did not mix but simply forced the Indian back. Whenever two civilizations meet, one or the other becomes predominant, but they both undergo a change; they both lose certain traits and win others. What had happened to the Spaniard during the Arab invasion, happend to the Indian during the Spanish invasion. There is a difference of course in degree, and, as we shall see later, a very surprising difference in results; but the truth is that the social, the historical process of the Spanish conquest in Mexico was about the same as any of the Asiatic or European invasions by which one race obtained control and predominance and the other

The case of North America, as we all know, was very different. This was not an invasion, at least not a sudden overwhelming invasion, but a long penetration of the territory without conservation of the native stock, and consequently without social contact or any other relation with the Indian.

This difference is the origin of the policy and of the practice of what we may call the one-race standard as against the mixed-race standard. By that I mean this undeniable fact that the civilization of North America is a one-race civilization, a white-race civilization as you insist on calling yourselves, sometimes even to the exclusion of other whites such as for instance the Spaniard. A white civilization that may contain, and does contain, millions of other racial stocks such as the Negro, but does not consider such dissimilar stock as a part of itself and does not as a rule intermarry with it. The Negro here, as well as the Indian, is in a world apart socially and is a body that is connected only politically with the white population. At any rate, the English have always stood for the conservation of an original unmixed human stock and have succeeded in maintaining it; and the Spaniards have always disregarded this purely white prejudice and have actually created the millions of the mestizo stock of America and of the Philippines. The unlimited progress of the Anglo-Saxons in civilization in the late centuries has led to the belief in the soundness of their policy. Many even among ourselves felt as though we had to admit that racial hybridism could only produce a low type of humanity, an inferior stock that could only be aided by the constant increase of a renewed wave of white people such as has flooded for instance the unpopulated territories of Argentina. Such is still the belief of some Latin-Americans of the purer European stock; and there are many mestizos who feel that all of their vows should favor the elimination of the native strain.

To me this last attitude is nothing but a case of cowardice. We are dazzled by the power of the world's civilization at the present moment; and we

forget that there is in hybridism something deeper than can be observed by the superficial thinker. It is very easy to agree with success; it is very easy to philosophize with the partial conclusions of a given moment of history. But if we think with our heads and if we search with our own souls into the mysteries of human destiny, we immediately find that the so-called pure-race theory is nothing but the theory of the dominating people of every period of history. And the pure-race theory leads, very often almost fatally, to the parental marriages, to the incestuous marriages of the Pharaos. If we observe human nature closely we find that hybridism in man, as well as in plants, tends to produce better types and tends to rejuvenate those types that have become static. If we go through history we find that after a period of adaptation the results of the renewal of blood are always beneficial. Even some of the most modern authors on the subject, such as the Frenchman Pittard, conclude that a pure race is a myth because all nations are the result of numerous mixtures; he goes farther and states that, "it is only the poor devils" that can claim a nonmixed pedigree, because only the lower classes amongst retired nations marry between themselves, while the powerful of any group always enrich their experience by marrying some of the prettiest or

more attractive women of the neighboring tribe. The advantages of a mixture of races has then been generally recognized; race prejudice as it exists today is a comparatively modern feeling and originates perhaps from the necessity for the English colonizer of far-distant territories densely populated by dissimilar races. The Englishman with his highly developed social instinct understood that if his colonizers of the distant territory of the Indies began to marry the native women, very soon the European in them would become absorbed and the next generation would become lost for the Empire. The Spaniards were bolder; for the decision to accept the results of intermarrying the native was not without forethought, but perfectly calculated, as is shown by the long discussion that preceded the papal statement to the effect that the Indians were entitled to receive the sacraments of the church, marriage among them.

Now if we compare the results of the English policy in India with the results of the Spanish policy in America, even at a time when the British policy is not yet ripe, not having had the centuries of the Spanish colonization's experience, I believe • that we shall be justified in declaring that the cultural results of the Spanish method are superior. The Spanish have succeeded in reproducing their

The English, on the other hand, with their system of not even maintaining social intercourse with the natives of India are today as completely strangers in India as on the day their ancestors first landed; and it does not seem probable that they will ever succeed in eradicating the Indian, to substitute for him the Islander. We find in India a coexistence, a juxtaposition, of cultures that do not mix, in the same manner that the bodies of the two races remain fatally apart. Which system is ultimately the better, even from the selfish point of view of the conqueror, is something that only the future can answer. We do not pretend to give advice, but we are bound to accept the Spanish method which in a way has created our nationalities and is the very reason of our existence as a race and a branch of the human family. We cannot condemn the Spanish method without denying ourselves.

The founders of the United States were fortunate in not finding in this territory a very large Indian population, and so it was easy for them to push the Indian back; but the importation of the Negro has brought to this nation, as we all know,

And the North Americans, who are the result of a mixture of all the European races, have followed the English system in regard to the Negro, that is to say, the system of strict avoidance of matrimonial relations with the colored race. The Spaniards did not obey this rule of abstention even with the Negro, the population of many of our tropical sections is largely mulatto, a mixture of the Spanish and the Negro; the Portuguese have also created a mulatto population in Brazil; so here again, we find the Latin system of assimilation and intermarriage and mixture opposed to the Anglo-Saxon method of matrimonial taboos and pure-race standards.

If the tremendous problem involved in this coexistence of two races that live apart in the same home could be considered as finally solved in this country, then there would be a good argument for those who claim that Mexico should try to do away with the mestizo and the Indian population by importing millions of Europeans; but the fact is that you cannot destroy a race, that you cannot change social conditions at will, and that you have to face the problem not only with the brain but also with the heart and the superior instinct of nature. And at least for us in Mexico, it is too late to change our

practices. There is nothing left for us to do, but to follow the Spanish tradition of eliminating the prejudice of color, the prejudice of race in all of our social procedures. No matter what our theoretical opinions might be, we have to start from the fact that the mestizo is the predominating element in Mexico.

But the mestizo of Mexico does not hold an undisputed reign over the country. Once in a while you still hear in Mexico an echo of the Indian voice that claims for a return to the past of the race as a means of obtaining strength and inspiration. The claims of the pure Indian sound sometimes almost as distinct in its vision as the creed of the most ardent advocate of the purity of the white in his own country. And the evidence that this is not merely a theoretical feeling is found in the story of our revolutions, which in some cases have developed purely Indian movements with the tendency to reinstate purely Indian standards. The Indian uprisings of Yucatan, known as guerra de castas, the "War of Castes," the pure Indian against the mestizo and the criollo, against the Spanish-speaking, against the Mexican population, is an old but clear example. The Zapata movement of the last revolution clearly contained the seeds of an Indian revival over the whole ex-

There was a time when the European dress was not allowed in the Zapata territory; and those Mexicans of white Spanish skin that happened to join the Zapata armies had to adopt the dress and the manners of Indian, in a certain way had to become indianized before they could be accepted. But the weakness of the pure Indian movement lies of course in the fact that the Indian has no civilized standards upon which to fall back. He has no language of his own, never had a language common to all of the race. And the advantage of the Spanish method of colonization through assimilation is here again demonstrated, I believe, in the fact that the Spanish spirit is still gaining victories over the native Indian spirit through its language, through its religion, and its social forms of living. Even the more radical leaders of the Zapata revolt, pure Indians like the school-teacher Montaño who was the brains of the group, were expressing themselves in good Spanish and were basing their economic theory of the distribution of the land, etc., on the terms of the European manner of life. At the same time the mass of the revolted Indians was carrying, as in the days of Hidalgo, the image of the Virgin of Guadalupe as a banner. They were being Spaniards even against their will and their knowledge; and it is only nat-

On the other hand, the movement, although very conscientiously prepared, was overpowered by the stronger mestizo element of the armies of Carranza, of Obregón, and of Villa, who represented the Mexican rather than the Indian, that is to say, the Spanish American, the Indo-Spaniard who also prevails in Central America, in Peru, and in Bolivia.

You may ask if the criollo, the descendant of the pure Spanish blood, has not also tried to gain control in Mexico, and I believe we can answer that the criollo has made efforts to predominate and also has failed in such efforts. I believe that such tendencies as those represented by the group that aligned itself with Maximilian were mainly European, Spanish tendencies. And to defeat them, the mestizo and the Indian joined under the leadership of the Indian Benito Juarez. Since that time I am sure that the alliance between the mestizo and the Indian has become final, and of course, there being no dividing line by reason of color or caste, the criollo also has entirely adapted himself to a national sentiment, or rather to a continental sentiment of Ibero-Americanism, that has as a common indestructible bond the Spanish language and the Spanish type of culture.

The truth is then that whether we like it or not the mestizo is the dominant element of the Latin-American continent. His characteristics have been pointed out many a time: a great vivacity of mind; quickness of understanding, and at the same time an unsteady temperament; not too much persistence in purpose; a somewhat defective will. It is curious to note that the blending of two different souls through inheritance has produced broader mental disposition. From a purely intellectual point of view I doubt whether there is a race with less prejudice, more ready to take up almost any mental adventure, more subtle, and more varied than the mestizo, or half-breed. I find in these traits the hope that the mestizo will produce a civilization more universal in its tendency than any other race of the past. Whether it is due to our temperament or to the fact that we do not possess a very strong national tradition, the truth is that our people are keen and are apt to understand and interpret the most contradictory human types. We feel the need of expressing life through many channels, through a thousand channels; we are not addicted to local tradition or to the European, but we desire to know and to try all—-the East and the West, the North and the South. A plurality of emotion, an almost mad desire to try all and to live

life from every point of view and every manner of sense experience —we are perhaps more truly universal in sentiment than any other people. Notwithstanding, we sometimes appear to be bigoted and patriotically local, but this is a result of the dangerous political position in which we have been placed in recent years. On the other hand we are unstable, and this I believe can be easily understood by the biologist, as we are a new product, a new breed, not yet entirely shaped. I believe such weakness can be overcome by obtaining a clear definition of our aims and by devoting ourselves to a definite and a great task.

Many of our failings arise from the fact that we do not know exactly what we want. First of all, then, we ought to define our own culture and our own purposes, and educate ourselves to them. No nation has ever risen to true greatness without an ardent faith in some high ideal. Democracy and equal opportunities for every man has been the motto of the great American nation. Broadness, universality of sentiment and thought, in order to fulfil the mission of bringing together all the races of the earth and with the purpose of creating a new type of civilization, is, I believe, the ideal that would give us in Latin-America strength and vision.

The more ambitious the goal is, the more strenuous the effort becomes. Our mystic temperament demands a task that has, in itself at least, a tendency that is unbounded and almost impossible. A fiery impetus is our only hope if we are to catch up with the world. Our struggle is, in a way, the struggle of the future, because every day mankind will come more and more into contact, and mixtures of all sorts of blood and thought and sentiment will go on increasing, and with them the phenomena and the problems of "mestisage" (of mixture) will become universal. The time and the opportunity of the one-blood, a pure-blood, group is passing away; everywhere the pure-blood groups are being absorbed; and even if they have been masters, they will not stand long before the increasing wave of the technically educated masses of the complex breed. In a way, the world is coming back to the confusion of Babel, and there will come a long period where mixture, or what we call "mestisage," is bound to be the rule. We cannot then dispose of the problem by declaring, as the evolutionists of the Spencer school declared, that the hybrid type was a degenerate type.

Such a statement represents only the short-sighted opinion of prejudice and the view advantageous to the imperialist. Let us remember though, that all imperialisms have been swallowed and overwhelmed exactly by those same masses they despised. The future will have to be prepared for in a different manner if we wish to see not a repetition of history but a new era in human progress.

From our local point of view in Mexico, I have started to preach the gospel of the mestizo by trying to impress on the minds of the new race a consciousness of their mission as builders of entirely new concepts of life. But if the mixed race is going to be able to do anything at all, it is first necessary to give it moral strength and faith in its own ability. The sort of science we have been teaching in our schools was not fit for this purpose; on the contrary it was the science created to justify the aims of the conqueror and the imperialist—the science that came to the help of the strong in their conquest and exploitation of the weak: the aristocracy of the white man and the empire of the white over the world, not only in the name of might but also on the ground of a certain semiscientific theory of the survival and predominance of the fittest, which is popularly known as Nietzschean evolutionism, from Nietzsche, the German. One of the first steps

Modern scientific theories are in many cases like the religious creeds of the old days, simply the intellectual justification of fatalities of conquest and of commercial greed. If all nations then build theories to justify their policies or to strengthen their deeds, let us develop in Mexico our own theories; or at least, let us be certain, that we choose among the foreign theories of thought those that stimulate our growth instead of those that restrain it. And so, instead of taking up, for instance, the Indian problem with the point of view of the ethnologist of the evolutionistic school who starts from the parti pris of his theory, according to which it will take the Indian about five thousand years to develop up to the mentality of the white, we ought to open our eyes to the fact that the Indian five thousand years ago was building monuments that the mentality of the white is using this very day as an inspiration for its new wonder cities of Chicago and New York. The ra-

Some races chiefly develop artistic ability; other people develop commercial aptitude; and so on. The conclusion of this theory would then be extremely favorable to the mestizo type of culture, as it tends to complement the weaknesses of a particular stock through interchange and assimilation with all the world. In fact, all of the great periods of history have been the work of a mixture of races, of peoples and cultures, rather than the work of any privileged pure-blood nation.

The policy of segregation of the races and the policy of educating the Indian or any other race of our Latin-American countries according to separate standards of any sort, is not only absurd among us, but it would be fatal. It would be doubly fatal because we do not have that element of pure racial stock that could undertake in our land that supposed leadership that has been taken here by the New Englander. Even if it should be true that this unmixed white leadership has created the greatness of the United States, we do not have in Mexico anything equivalent to this root. Our prog-

ress may have been delayed on this account, but there is nothing left for us to do except to go ahead with the situation that nature has made for us; and instead of a blind, shortsighted copying of methods, we should go along creating what is required by our own problem and mission. For us, there is only one sound race policy and that is the policy of old-the policy of the Spaniard and the Christian who took it for granted that we are all potentially the same and that we are bound to respond differently according to the call that is made upon us, each one bearing a treasure that comes to life at the proper moment in the time of need. The duty of any great culture is, then, to raise human beings as a whole and to call races together so that they can all collaborate in the task of a truly material and spiritual civilization. Do not put a man to a task for which he is not fitted. Place every one in his own vocation. Bring down to social practical life a little of the rule of joy instead of the hard, blind rules of need and of duty, and you will transform the world. But if we are ever going to approach any realization of this sort, we must start first by transforming all of our theories. For it is in the soul where the germ of our disease should be sought, in the mind, and more particularly in the heart. So long as our ideals do not correspond to our intimate

p. 99

wish and desire, our ideals will not be worthwhile. So long as pleasure and joy do not become the rule of life, we must admit that we are lost in a mistaken path. Duty is a means, knowledge is a means, effort is a means; joy and pleasure are the only ends. And there can be no joy in a civilization where races are separated by hatred, prejudice, and misunderstanding.

I have said that humanity is going back to Babel and by this I mean that the day of the isolated civilization is over. In this new coming-together of all the races we ought not to repeat the methods of the past, the methods that transformed Babel into a curse. Babel became a curse because the different peoples did not understand each other and consequently, instead of concurring in a common purpose, they entered into competition and jealousy that destroyed every one of them. It is time then that we should change our methods in order to make the new period of Babel a prosperous one. The whole theory of the superiority of one stock above the other will have to be changed. The practice of putting the so-called lower races to work for the benefit of the superior will have to be abandoned or else the dominators will have to suffer from the revenge of the oppressed. This revenge is found in the law of nature itself. Notice

The Spanish civilization is perduring today in America, not on account of the pure descendants of the conqueror but because of the mixed race and the Indian race that were educated and assimilated by the Spaniard. If the Spaniard had not mixed his blood with the Indian, there would not be today on the map a large area of countries where the Spanish soul is alive and progressive. When, on the contrary, the dominating race stands apart and takes no interest in the life of the inferior, the inferior tends instinctively to increase its numbers in order to compensate through numbers what the dominating race achieves through quality. The more civilized a nation is, the more it reduces its reproduction, the tendency being to obtain an advantage in quality. But the lower, opposed breed, having no control, no hope, goes on multiplying madly; and the weight and the curse of this overpopulation is just as harmful to the elect as it is to the less fortunate. If we are ever going to stop this misery, it is necessary that the superior take pains to educate the inferior and to raise its standard. If we do not wish to be over-

whelmed by the wave of the Negro, of the Indian, or of the Asiatic, we shall have to see that the Negro, the Indian, and the Asiatic are raised to the higher standards of life, where reproduction becomes regulated and quality predominates over numbers. Instead of the competitive manner of life advocated by the defenders of the pure-race civilization and by the imperialists and conquerors, we shall have to adopt, then, the co-operative, collaborating manner of interracial organization; instead of the struggle for life and the survival of the fittest, a collaboration of all human efforts for the production of a variety in quality—a salvation brought about, not through the elect of a certain type more or less strong, but through the utilization of every particular aptitude for the purpose of creating superior values. If the mad competition and distrust of the present day can be overcome, every race will then be able to work for the achievement of quality and no race will devote itself to merely creating numbers; thereby the present-day danger of the overpowering of the superior few by the uncivilized many, will disappear.

If we thus turn selfish competition into farsighted collaboration, it will not even matter much that we follow the pure-race, the one-race standard or the mixed-race standard. The very same dif-

Spiritual affinities and similar fancies of taste and mind will then prevail, and a superior life will become the endeavor of the human family as a whole.

INCORPORATING THE INDIAN IN THE MEXICAN POPULATION

Mexico is a country of complicated social phenomena, and the true explanation of this lies in the diverse racial, cultural, and mental contacts between the different white and indigenous groups of inhabitants.

The evolution of the aboriginal peoples of Mexico was quite normal in nearly all respects. From their immigration in post-Neolithic times to the Conquest, they underwent a long process of adaptation to the physical conditions of the land, and throughout those hundreds of years were subjected to a prolonged selective process. Their civilization was their own and not inherited, for, according to the latest investigators, these peoples had no contact with contemporary Asiatic or European cultures after their arrival in America. This civilization was spontaneous, that is, it grew from progressive, convergent mental development, from geographic and biologic influence. For this reason their racial characteristics were normal, their cultural manifestations logical, and their social struc-

p. 106

ture natural and properly organized. Furthermore, one can state that, generally speaking, the pre-Hispanic peoples of Mexico were notable for their relative cultural and ethnic unity.

At the end of the fifteenth century Spain did not have, and could not have, the abilities and qualities of a colonizing nation, as is clearly demonstrated by the dismal results of Spanish-Indian contact during the colonial period—a fundamental cause today of the deficient development of Indo-Hispanic peoples. The Spanish people did not exist, at the time, either nationally or sociologically, for the inhabitants of the Iberian peninsula were not held together by racial, linguistic, or traditional bonds. A multiplicity of languages and dialects, heterogeneous cultural characteristics, antagonistic and divergent national concepts —all these kept the social elements of the country in constant struggle. There would have been far more progress for Spain, had it begun to devote itself to the task of nationalization and general identification at that time, instead of launching itself upon the adventure of conquering a whole continent which, in the end, was destined to absorb much precious energy that might have been better spent at home. Furthermore, the Spanish colonizers were, with rare exceptions, soldiers of fortune with scarcely any

p. 107

knowledge or comprehension of the work laid out before them.

Spain has been the home, for countless centuries, of many peoples, some of which are known to us only by their archaeological traces. She suffered incessant series of invasions and immigrations of peoples differing in race, culture, and speech from those who came before them and with whom they eventually came in contact. Briefly, the Spanish population at the time of the Conquest included: Basques, Iberians, Celts, Celtiberians, Phoenicians, Carthaginians, 'Romans, Vandals, Swabians, Visogoths, and Mohammedans; and these widely diverging groups had struggled upon the Spanish peninsula for fifteen centuries. At the beginning of the sixteenth century, Spain was in the grip of numerous and significant social movements. The warrior and adventurer classes could not find a place in a peaceful régime, after taking part in the prolonged conflicts of conquest and re-conquest; the Musselman culture was having a harmful disintegrating effect; a fanatic religious struggle held Christians, Jews, and Mohammedans in deadlock; several regions had their individual habits, customs, traditions, languages, or dialects, and there were therefore various divergent nationalistic tendencies: those of the Navarres, Catalans, Valen-

p. 108

cians, and Galicians. Ferdinand and Isabella, realizing this, began the labor of unification or creation of the Spanish nation; and probably their plan might have been brilliantly successful had not the American colonies distracted and interfered with it.

In the American territories where the native population was numerically insignificant and therefore easily absorbed or annihilated, the Spanish newcomers faced only the task of social unification already begun at home, and of acclimating and adapting themselves to the new land. Such was the case in Argentina, where the Spanish immigrants were relatively heterogeneous, yet came racially and culturally together and developed what might be called the standard Argentinian type. Another favorable circumstance contributing to this successful colonization was the orographic, climatic, and botanical unity of the greater part of that country.

The situation was quite different for the colonists in the countries whose native population was extraordinarily dense, as in Mexico, which even yet bears evident proof of this; for in proportion to its area it probably contains more archaeological remains than any other territory on the globe. Mexico is also a country of great orographic and climatic differences and even contrasts, and this was an-

p. 109

other difficulty for the colonists—a circumstance which would in any case have been an obstacle to social unification, without the added difficulty of resistance on the part of the natives.

Although the Spanish invaders annihilated a large proportion of the native population, their number was insignificant when compared to the enormous masses of these people, all of whom were finally subdued. The latent hostility and profound enmity of the two races was increased by each manifestation of national spirit on the part of the conqueror. From his point of view, the intelligent and convenient thing would have been to placate his subjects with generous concessions of advantage to both, as those classic colonizers, the Romans, did in several cases. But instead, he began by doubting the physiological relationship of the people who had constructed the marvels of Maya and Toltec architecture, and granted them a place in the human family only as long as the efforts of a pious decen and the decision of a judicious prelate so

The immediately succeeding racial contact, upon conquest, had no ethical, social, or eugenic tendencies, but was exclusively physical. The white man possessed the native woman wherever and whenever he saw fit. Therefore the offspring

p. 110

of these inharmonious and forced unions had none of the advantages of a normal origin. Moreover, the mestizo, or half-breed, born under these circumstances was educated by the mother, since the father abandoned the woman sooner or later, and he in turn increased in body and spirit the indigenous masses—passive enemies of the white colonists.

The cultural contact between the Spanish colonists and the native population is of great sociological significance, and deserving of special attention. Immediately upon the conquest of the Indian came his economic ruin; his lands and all valuable possessions passed into the hands of the invader, and he himself became the servant of his conqueror, obliged to work in his mines or estates or pay him tribute. With the Indian effectively subjugated, the Spaniard thought it would be an easy task to blot out the remnant of his autochthonous culture and substitute the civilization imported from Spain. His plan, however, met with utter failure. He first attempted to soften the bloody and essentially mercenary character of the conquest by means of the implantation of Catholicism, but this the Indians resisted tenaciously, continuing in their adoration, more or less veiled, of the old gods. Thereupon the Inquisition began to function as an

p. 111

agent of conversion, with such cruelty that the Spanish court itself became horror-stricken and decreed that never again should the Indian be subjected to the activities of this terrible tribunal. This religious conflict retarded the realization of one of the favorite projects of the colonial clergy, which was to make of the indigenous masses the economic foundation of the Church's future development. The method adopted finally, in the face of these difficulties, was that of transportation or transformation. Into the mythology were incorporated the exterior and attractive modalities of Catholicism, out of which there developed a strange hybrid of superstition and idolatrous religious concepts, very far in essence from the principles of Roman Catholicism. The hybrid religion flourished and developed all over the country and gathered such strength during the colonial period that today it constitutes the religion of ten million Mexican Indians and half-breeds.

From the contact with the aesthetic ideas of conqueror and conquered, the native artist suffered great loss, for the fanatic apostle and the destructive invader threw themselves into the task of completely razing pre-Hispanic architecture and with it, sculpture-the apostle, to weaken the strong influence of the mythological beliefs whose per-

p. 112

sonages he found beautifully sculptured in temple and palace; and the soldier, to prevent the renaissance of the emotion-quickening art of the Indian which might fan the fire of rebellion in the conquered people. By superhuman effort the Indians protected their gods from this brutal vandalism by burying the pyramids and temples under thousands of tons of earth, as is demonstrated to modern archaeologists. But the artist suffered more than all the other native classes, for, deprived of his religious impulse which, wedded to the purely plastic, had produced so many objects of beauty, he was now forced to follow only his aesthetic predispositions, and produced creations which were less expressive, individualistic, and dramatic.

The mythology of the natives concerned itself with a number of divinities who were identified with natural objects and who presided over all cosmic and human activities. It was a pantheon which considered and included planets, earth, comets, earthquakes, agriculture, commerce, industry, war, love, prostitution, intoxication, sport, birth, and death. In this Olympic program the gods had duties more comprehensive and varied than the facul ty of any great and modern university. Entirely contrary to Catholicism, this religion did not demand unconditional adoration of a god merely be-

p. 113

cause he was a god, nor did it make provision for the ethical and moral uplift of humanity, nor did it present a much-feared Satan and his sinister abode, in spite of all the efforts of the religious chroniclers in these directions when the religious fusion, already defined, began. Ethics, the good social and personal behavior, was considered to a certain extent a biological function; and crimes or sins which harmed the community were given radical treatment. For instance, drunkenness was represented by four hundred gods on the Aztec Olympus, and yet these virile people were not addicted to this vice, but on the contrary fought it to the point of imposing capital punishment for it—a wise legislation which did away effectively with abuse of drink, a thing permitted only to the old, who after long and laborious life might freely refresh their lips with the foaming pulque, or native beer.

This mythology made deities of the material objects and natural forces from which humanity derived life and nourishment; and this affords an explanation of the spiritual significance of the reproduction of these concepts by the Indian, constituting his own interpretation of the deity affiliated with the theme. In other words, native art was not art for art's sake, nor did it awaken in the observer aesthetic emotions only; it was a thing symbolic

p. 114

and aesthetic, for with the pleasure of the beautiful, it associated mentally the objects and active divinities of personal, social, and cosmic life.

The fusion of native mythology, fount of inspiration, with Catholicism adulterated the artistic production of the people, for the pagan Catholic images of saints or divine entities no longer provoked the powerful response of human life to nature, nor offered majestic and objective creative themes, but they bore a creation entangled in semiabstract concepts and the colorless contemplation of anonymous personages. For these reasons, and because the development of the art of native architecture was paralyzed-and with it, native sculpture the Indian could only cultivate the minor arts; and his labor was done in the light of a purely aesthetic criterion, for symbolism died with his brilliant and picturesque mythology. To this class of posthumous production belong the costuming, pottery-making, lacquering, etc., of post-Hispanic and modern Indians and half-breeds. However, in some of the native families which had, and continue to have, little contact with the whites, there is a marked persistence of mythical concepts; and their decorations bear the same originality and strength as did the pre-Hispanics.

Besides mythology proper, the professional un-

p. 115

derstanding of which belonged to the priests, there were popular interpretations of the same and other natural objects and activities—a sort of pre-Hispanic folklore, which, only slightly influenced, has been handed down from Indian father to son wherever they have remained untouched and primitive. Mixed with the popular culture the Spaniards brought, folklore is the pivot of the mental life of our half-breeds. The same might be said of the music and dances.

The task of urbanizing the recalcitrant Indians, with the object of subjecting them to the yoke, was one which took a good deal of the time and thought of colonial authorities, who compelled them to concentrate in villages or colonies and furthermore forced them to lay out these settlements according to the Spanish plan, that is, one house next to another and the streets marking blocks geometrically parallel. Some Indian tribes lived in inaccessible regions and refused to come out; others sought refuge in them, and are still there, as for instance the Mayas and Lacandones of upper Usumacinta and the Huicholes of Tepic and Jalisco. The more submissive ones gathered in pueblos, preserving as far as possible their traditional and picturesque city arrangement, with irregular streets, their houses standing alone in a

p. 116

garden or vegetable patch, making the whole seem like a garden city. Even today, most Indian pueblos are laid out in this picturesque and hygienic fashion; whereas those in which the Spanish plan was made absolutely compulsory are today unaesthetic and hybrid clusters of houses, inhabited under conditions hardly healthy for any community.

As to food, a satisfactory fusion of the two systems was not achieved here, either. The Indians continued their almost exclusively vegetarian diet: corn, in liquid and solid form, beans, vegetables and chili; for bread, meat, and other foods were far too expensive for them. Pulque was the drink of the people throughout part of the Central Plateau, and coffee became so on the coasts. The colonists introduced highly alcoholic drinks and their use became general throughout the country, causing much harm.

No really scientific investigation has been made of the physiological effects of such a diet, but experimental observations have made the fact evident that the resistance of the Indians to tasks requiring muscular energy is satisfactorily comparable to that of the white, if one takes into account the very small rations of the Indian. Moreover, the exceedingly good condition of the Indians'

p. 117

teeth is often cited, and this has been maintained without the use of dentifrices.

Native laws, as other native elements, were adapted to their historical antecedents and to the material and intellectual life of their subjects, for they were the results of centuries of experiment. Murder, robbery, adultery, commercial dishonesty, and other misdemeanors were effectively punished, but not because they were considered acts offensive to the gods, for we have already spoken of the drunken gods, and the deities of prostitution. Rather, they were considered biological abnormalities and the laws purposed to counteract their effect. The civil, religious, and military institutions functioned regularly, thanks to a disciplined and logical organization. Even their incessant wars and bloody ceremonies obeyed not precisely an unconscious desire for self-destruction, but inevitable impulses which at bottom are identical or analogous to those which cause the modern Mexican revolutions. The irregular and difficult living conditions in various regions forced their inhabitants to move and to drive out the people they found in more fertile and productive spots; and this was one of the chief causes of war.

The colonists attempted to destroy this legislation at one blow and to make the Indian recog-

p. 118

nize and accept the laws imported from Spain, which to him were utterly exotic. No one dreamed of examining the native laws with a view to selecting the best of them and incorporating them into his own legal code in order to form a mixed but satisfactory set of laws, protective of the needs and interests of both. The Crown used a judgment somewhat more sound than did its colonial subjects, for it projected the famous "Laws of the Indies," which, unfortunately, were scarcely ever put in effect, for as a matter of fact there was no law in New Spain other than that to the material or other interest of the white colonists. The native masses sank to a mechanical, dark, and painful existence, broken by occasional brief surges of protest and hate for the oppressors.

Curiously enough, one contact was successfully achieved; and this was the linguistic. Caused by as yet unknown phenomena, the native languages and dialects gradually became part of the tongue of the conquerors, until Spanish spoken in Mexico today is a thing full of Indianisms; and it is spoken by everybody except the million and a half Indians who speak exclusively their native tongues. This circumstance has caused an enormous error in the Mexican census wherein all Spanish-speaking persons are classified as white, whereas the great

p. 119

majority of these "whites" are Indian by characteristics much more important than language: those of race and culture.

Mexico achieved her independence from Spain in I821, but it was a movement which favored only the white minorities, for the indigenous masses remained in the same miserable situation, or perhaps worse, as during the colonial period. By the new constitution they lost the only support they had, the weak Laws of the Indies. This constitution proclaimed equal rights for all inhabitants, but it did not establish any means of making these rights effective or beneficial to the Indian. All relationships between the two ethnic groups continued under the same evil auspices begun after the Conquest. The inter-breeding was still a very abnormal and unsocial thing. Its defective genesis made it a convergence of the bad in the two races, not the good.

So unstable and artificial a situation could not but culminate in revolution, in the incessant revolutions which tore Mexico for one hundred years after its Proclamation of Independence. Some of these revolutions tended exclusively toward the betterment of the white or partly half-breed minorities, counting to gain their ends with the unconscious aid of the natives, who in the final reckoning

p. 120

got nothing at all for their pains. Other wars, such as the bloody, dreadful rebellions called "wars of caste," were the explosion of Indian ancestral rage, directed without mercy against the white invader; but since the white had all the advantages of battle given him by modern civilization, these last flares of native republican indignation were smothered and extinguished with as much blood as that spilled by the monarchical swords of Cortes and Alvarado.

The later revolutions have been more logical, because they have had their origin in the longings for betterment of both white and Indian, who after four hundred years of butchery have at last got within sight of their goal, which is an economic one. They have laid aside their arms, but nevertheless continue struggling, at present with the recognition and encouragement of a revolutionary president, General Calles; and they are struggling toward ideals not less important than the economic, such as the racial, cultural, psychological, and nationalistic impulses, the latter a combination after a fashion of the former.

The triumph of a revolution that was both military and political has brought with it an economic victory which includes synthetically the betterment of wages and consequently better living conditions for the city workers, who are whites and

p. 121

half-breeds; has brought also the division of the great latifundios, or absentee-owned estates, and the formation of small agrarian property, measures benefiting the indigenous masses chiefly; this is reflected favorably in the attitude of the middle and capitalist classes, who have recognized that their own well-being varies in direct ratio to that of the workers. This triumph has made possible the rebirth of national industry, commerce, and agriculture.

It will take longer to satisfy these racial, cultural, and spiritual necessities already mentioned. Once the economic problem is solved, there still remains that of making favorable ethnic-social contacts, which we have already mentioned as having been done on the spur of the moment and defectively, from the colonial period to date. The ethnic fusion continues, producing an undesirable interbreeding under conditions unfavorable to the effective development of the Indian. From a purely materialistic point of view, cultural contacts have taken place, but when these are not accompanied by amalgamation with abstract cultural concepts, they are merely superficial and artificial. For instance, the Ford, the sewing machine, the phonograph come heralding the modern civilization and penetrate to the most remote Indian villages. It

p. 122

is not enough, however, to provide the Indians with modern machinery; an understanding of their mental attitudes, their hopes and aspirations, is essential to an effective substitution of the instruments and institutions of modern civilization, or to a fusion of the modern and the primitive. Unless a substitution or a fusion takes place, industrial instruments will have no cultural dynamic influence. They will have been merely passive additions to Indian life, purely useful to a greater or lesser degree. The same thing happened to the wagon, the wooden plow, the pick and shovel, the hat and trousers, which the conqueror brought and which, if they did save the Indian muscular effort or change his original garments into something of more conventional modesty, yet they nevertheless left him sunken for several centuries in the bitterest cultural misery.

The aspirations of race, culture, and spirit are palpably revealed by simple observation of the Mexican people. The successful realization of these aspirations depends upon the proper contacts. It would seem, at first sight, a fairly simple thing to begin at once to direct and foster those contacts, but this is difficult, for the reason that it is impossible to normalize the relations between two social groups when the physical, material, and

p. 123

intellectual characteristics of one or both are unknown—a thing which is unfortunately true of the Mexican people and especially with respect to the indigenous majority.

Some time ago an investigation was made under our direction, which consisted in selecting and grouping synthetically all the authoritative conclusions reached by foreign or Mexican students, concerning the indigenous families of Mexico. From diagrams it is clearly shown that the actual information about these tribes is practically nil, too superficial and insufficient in any case to serve as a basis for the successful promotion of future contacts between them and the white elements.

In the first place, the conditions under which the muscular development of the Indian and halfbreed takes place are unknown, and the only conclusions are drawn from an insignificant number of incomplete anthropometric and physiological studies available up to the present. Indeed, the natives whose anthropometric measurements have been taken by scientific methods do not number four thousand, and such a number is unsignificant as against the eight or ten millions of Indians and half-breeds whose anatomical characteristics remain unknown. If, at least, one could count upon a satisfactory number of physiological observa-

p. 124

tions, the insufficiency of those measurements would not matter so much; since physiological information is of more value in racial characterization than anatomical; but, unfortunately, the determination of the physiological constants we could count upon are even fewer and more faulty than the measurements already mentioned. As to pathological data, there are scarcely any at all.

The hereditary factors and those of adaptation and selection are bound to make a great difference between the Indian, native of the environment for thousands of years, and the white, relatively a newcomer of other ethnic sources; pathologically, anatomically, physiologically these differences are especially great. This ignorance of the Indian's physical mechanism has carried over into Mexican medicine and even into hygienic legislation the same error which has characterized education, that is, the use of methods of foreign origin which, if applicable to the white minorities, are not so successful when applied to the indigenous and halfbreed majorities. That is why, contrary to expectation, there are not among us schools of medicine for tropical diseases; nor, in those which we have, is there any special attention given to noteworthy phenomena, such as local or practically local diseases. One of these diseases is the mal del pinto,

p. 125

which causes a polychromatic pigmentation of the skin and which affects the inhabitants of an extensive southern area of the country, where is also found afilaria (choroiditis), a disease which causes blindness and which has recently been identified and studied by an eminent Guatemaltecan doctor. The bocio (goiter), an exaggerated glandular development in the neck, affects numerous inhabitants of central and southern Mexico; a peculiar dark coloring appears upon the teeth of people of Durango; certain mental derangements are caused by the peyote cactus among Indians of Durango, Chihuahua, and other northern states.

And if methods of scientific investigation have not been applied to native diseases, their cause and cure, neither do we know anything about the traditional pharmacopoeia or the empiric therapeutics and magic with which the Indian fights his ills.

As to studying the innumerable cultural aspects of the indigenous population, probably not twenty integral, truly scientifically methodological investigations exist. There are many pamphlets and even books upon such matters, but they are superficial; and, above all, the observations are made with the same criterion as that applied to the cultural phenomena of white populations, in spite of the fact that the psychological complex which moves a

p. 126

white man's mind is quite different from that which moves the native. All this lack of understanding, though with well-meant effort perhaps, makes the struggle between the two elements more bitter instead of less so; for the Indian, because of his cultural inferiority, has the right to expect the white to understand his peculiar ways of thinking since he cannot be expected to ascend mentally, rapidly, miraculously, to the plane of the difficult ideological and material mechanism which characterizes the modern civilization of the white minorities.

The lack of a scientific criterion is even more marked in most of the studies made about the aboriginal languages and dialects, precisely because these are carried on by methods used in the study of foreign, and especially of the classic, languages. The census demonstrates that Mexican native languages and dialects are dying out more or less quickly and will come to a natural end, so that we do not undertake their ample and intense investigation with the puerile end of hindering this decadence, which is beneficial to national unification. But, because these languages and dialects are the only path to the Indian's soul, we need some understanding of them. His native tongue expresses the Indian not only ideologically but also materially. The Indian who speaks only Spanish, or even who

p. 127

speaks both Spanish and his own language, can no longer give us, and would not know how, if he wished, the true and intimate phases of traditional thought and the culture with which he is by birth identified. He could neither name them nor describe them. He will think in Spanish and will involuntarily fall into the error of interpreting in this tongue an autochthonous soul.

It is unquestionably urgent, most urgent, to investigate the indigenous population of Mexico scientifically, for until this is done thoroughly, social contacts cannot be normalized and orientated authoritatively, a thing by all means desirable since it requires convergent racial, cultural, and spiritual fusion to secure unification of tongue and equilibrium of economic interests. This, and only this, can place the Mexican nation as a nation, upon a solid, logical, consistent, and permanent base.

II. THE EDUCATION OF INDO-HISPANIC PEOPLES

PEOPLES

In Mexico, the most representative of Indo-Hispanic countries, many different educational experiments have been tried during the past few years. Innumerable projects have been cast and re-cast, but scarcely anything of definite value has been accomplished; and this is due not to a lack of intelligence, learning, or enthusiasm on the part of the authors, but to the general unawareness of the educational or other needs of the dissimilar ethnicsocial groups which integrate the Mexican masses.

One frequently hears it said that "to educate a people is to create a nation." This holds good for groups whose members are already united by historic, traditional, racial, cultural, linguistic, and economic bonds; for such peoples, education merely complements and cements the nationalization already initiated and developed on these basic factors. A notable example of such a nation is Japan, whose inhabitants less than a century ago were not at all educated in the modern sense of the word but who who were moved by coherent national

p. 130

impulses and, guided by education, were transformed almost miraculously from a medieval country into a modern and important nation. Advanced pedagogical methods bore rapid and happy fruit among the Japanese because there was homogeneity, solidarity, and convergent aspirations throughout the national social strata. The new education was unanimously comprehended and embraced as the wedge that would lift the country into its proper place in the world.

But the outlook for modern education among the Indo-Hispanic peoples is entirely different. Here are groups heterogenous socially, racially, culturally, and linguistically, with pronounced lack of economic equilibrium. Ethnologically, there are the Indian, the half-breed, and the white—all more or less distinct and separate from each other, and also different in history and tradition. Socially, the Latin-American republics are made up of elements ranging from the most primitive to the ultra-modern; culturally, one finds many different stages of civilization. The principal cities are distinctly modern in their institutions; but outside of them live tribes so little advanced that they make use of practically no pottery or dress, and their ideas have felt the effect of little or no civilizing influence. The same great difference exists linguistically, for be-

p. 131

sides the two divisions of Spanish and native, the native division is subdivided into hundreds of languages and dialects. These discrepancies are paralleled economically. Tens of millions of men exist on miserable wages that vary from ten to fifty cents gold, while their millionaire countrymen live princely and useless lives abroad. To further complicate the situation, the governments of these Latin-American countries have nearly always been unstable, for the government, democratic in name only, has had to adapt itself successively and without distinction to the idiosyncracies and the manifestations of the widely varied social groups of the country. One group, exclusive of the rest, triumphed and dominated inevitably, until protest grew strong enough to instal some other power, and so on interminably.

Now what educational program could be expected to yield effective results in countries of the typically heterogenous structure here described? For a long time the only program followed at all, and still observed, though of course erroneously, has been that of selecting and applying methods which have proved successful elsewhere—in foreign countries utterly different, and above all far more advanced, as nations. Under this system, those small groups which, due to cultural antecedents—

p. 132

racial or psychic characteristics-bore some relationship to the foreigners whose methods were adopted, were partly benefited by the exotic transplantation. But the great Indian and half-breed majority gave no response whatever to these pedagogical innovations, for they were not molded nor adequate to the specific diversities of their cultural characteristics, their racial conditions, their peculiar mentalities, nor to their miserable economic condition; and this last was the chief shortcoming. These pariahs needed, and needed badly, not alphabets and theoretic postulates but something which could show them the way to get enough to eat. Education, for this enormous majority of the Latin-American people, is inseparably linked with economic betterment, and to attempt this it would be necessary to reform completely not only machinery and organization but especially the existing criterion.

One might almost have to start at the very beginning. For instance, it would be indispensable to take a true census, a true qualitative and quantitative evaluation of the inhabitants of Mexico, for those taken up to now have been done with slavish addiction to foreign methods. Consequently our population has been classified according to speech; and in the census published in 1918 by the

p. 133

Department of Statistics, I3,143,372 persons are listed as Spanish-speaking and 1,690,356 as non-Spanish-speaking-that is, as Indians. The conclusions to be drawn would be that Mexico is composed of a great white, civilized majority and a small native, culturally backward minority. Laws, educational dispositions, political systems, etc., were established on such a statistical basis, tending therefore toward the exclusive betterment of the supposed white, Spanish-speaking modern majority, which is in reality a very small minority, for most of the inhabitants of Mexico are native, racially and culturally, though a large portion of them speak Spanish. This prolonged official error has left more than ten million Indians and halfbreeds entirely abandoned to their ignorant and miserable living, for the projects to help and educate them, supposing them white, always proved exotic and quite useless. The white minority, exclusively benefited by systems designed to help the total population, became more and more the directing, monopolizing and tyrannical element.

In a study that I submitted to President Calles shortly before my connection with the Secretariat of Education, I suggested that, under the direction of a proposed Secretariat of Population, a thorough investigation be made of the true characteristics

⟦[[I33]⟧

p. 134

and aspirations of the widely different social groups of which our people consist. Upon the basis of such knowledge as this, one could initiate an authoritative, parallel education for our masses, with a very definite tendency toward their economic betterment. The first thing would be to take a census in order to obtain the real number and value of the various components of our population. The numbers so procured would not be mere data with no practical use whatsoever, but would serve as nothing less than the foundation for new activities, for they would indicate very definitely through the information on the proportion of illiteracy, vital statistics, industrial poverty, racial and cultural conditions, etc., where help was most urgently needed. Keeping in mind precisely the integral character and ultimate use of this information, the census would be supplemented by information on the racial characteristics. This census would give various aspects and would therefore suggest orientations for a methodical, careful investigation of the Mexican people, their needs and shortcomings, with the practical end, already indicated, of ministering to the first and correcting the second.

The preparatory data of the census would be a starting point for the scientific investigation of other phases of the life of the Mexican people:

p. 135

vegetable, animal and mineral elements at the disposal of the inhabitants in each region, especially in Indian territories. These investigations, like the others, would not be of an exclusively scientific character but would have specifically utilitarian ends and tendencies, designed to localize, Identity, and develop the raw materials which, by means of simple and efficacious methods, the Indians could transform into easily negotiable industrial products and into products which would contribute to their own physical and cultural needs. At the same time the highly important relation of geography to human life should be studied.

Generally speaking, to know the conditions under which a people lives it is indispensable to know those under which it developed in the past, for to a great extent, the present is to be explained by the past. This is especially true of a people such as the Mexican, formed of an indigenous majority which does not live on a modern plane of civilization but presents the same or a similar life to that during the Colonial period or even in pre-Historic times. Though a large part of our natives speak Spanish, their huts or jacales, their domestic utensils such as the metate (corn-grinder), the petate (leaf mat), the huarache (leather sandal), the haucal (crate for carrying produce), their pagan polythe-

p. 136

ism, their ideas of witchcraft and magic, etc., are almost identically those of several centuries ago. The archaeological-historical evolution of our native population is therefore a necessary postulate to the true comprehension of its living conditions today.

At present the white population of the country, smallest in number, develops under relatively satisfactory conditions, whereas the native and halfbreed generally suffer great moral and material wrongs, the origin of which lies in causes we should know but usually ignore, though their fatal effects could hardly be more patent: defective organic development, economic distress, illiteracy, isolation, retardation, etc.; and in view of this it seems logical to concentrate attention upon them.

After obtaining some data on geographic resources and archaeological-historical antecedents, it would be of special interest to investigate carefully the actual anatomical, physiological, and pathological conditions of our natives, as well as their peculiar social organization; their economic situation; religious, moral, and artistic ideas; commercial, industrial, and cultural phenomena.

Data that the census would give on density and distribution could be almost immediately applied for re-distribution wherever necessary or advisable.

p. 137

Auto-colonization of the sparsely populated regions would at the same time improve the working conditions in the places inhabited by a fairly large number of laborers and farmers. Such colonies could be distributed for educational purposes in places where the people were most backward, and this would mean the first step toward the agricultural and industrial development of regions at present desert or badly cultivated, to which Mexicans might be sent from regions unfavorable to proper development, due to poverty of natural resources, excess population, etc.

Upon the results of these investigations, which would without doubt give the true situation of our people, plans to normalize their development could be based. It is an exceedingly long task, and certain phases of it would prolong themselves indefinitely, so that it would be impossible to wait for its conclusion before beginning to educate the people. Parallel to investigation, therefore, education should begin to be implanted, always keeping in mind that any system adopted would not be related, except incidentally, to exclusively theoretic knowledge or pedagogy.

The fundamental tendency in the education of our Mexican masses should, I think, be directed toward teaching them the best means of making a

p. 138

living; how to develop and utilize the natural resources, fountains of life and well-being, which are so abundant in the cities and territories inhabited by them. In elaborating any system of education for Mexico, one should keep its geography well in mind, for, derived from the economic factor, prime in Mexican education is the topographic factor. The same things are not useful to everybody, and therefore, educationally Mexico falls into three geographic groups, and such studies as I have made of the problem, have been based on this fundamental classification: urban, rural, and coast region education.

URBAN EDUCATION

It is logical to suppose that we city dwellers know the sources of life and riches open to us. However, we do not. In the capital of Mexico, for instance, it would be hard to find any one person who could tell accurately and in detail the numerous and different ways by which a living might be made in the metropolis. The census and statistics should shed a good deal of light upon this aspect of city life, but unfortunately this information was not gathered with sufficient sociological perspective, and is therefore unsatisfactory.

If it is true that the adults do not know what there is around them, it is obvious that the children

p. 139

are even more ignorant in this respect. School teachers and parents are well acquainted with the vague outlook of a child at the end of his elementary, and sometimes even of his high-school, education. He does not know, he cannot choose, the field in which he can develop his capabilities, and this is the cause of many mistakes and failures and the beginning of far too many mediocre and passive lives.

This deplorable situation is due to the eminently abstract character of the education which the child receives; and the passive attitude is a result of scholastic life, regulated by an abstract criterion. For example: Suppose that a Mexican youth, graduate of some elementary city school, needs to work for wages sufficient to live on, a position in which go per cent of the children of the Mexico City populace find themselves. This boy, who has some theoretic and superficial knowledge-reading, writing, arithmetic, geography-discovers the discouraging fact that he can find no productive use for his knowledge. It is insufficient in itself to justify a remunerative employment. Furthermore, this boy is ignorant of the thousand ways and means by which a city-wage is earned, he does not know where to find work, he is not acquainted with its nature and characteristics. He merely senses the enormous and chaotic honeycomb of the me-

p. 140

tropolis. Finally, this boy does not possess a dynamically trained personality, but a passive one, molded in six years of confinement in his school. His vision is narrow and his curiosity as to what happens in the city—the true school, the school of real life-is dulled. After much drifting and many mistakes, this youth will find some sort of work, but rarely that wherein he could have used his faculties to best advantage. He might originally have had some inclination toward electricity and might have been a clever craftsman at it, or perhaps an active and enthusiastic clerk; but an abstract education and the very concrete necessity of making a living have driven him to be an illhumored chauffeur's apprentice or even a miserable newsboy.

It might be pointed out that there are elementary schools in Mexico City which teach, in addition to the abstract subjects already mentioned, experimental gardening, agriculture, small crafts, and industries. I sincerely respect the good faith and the pioneer spirit of the organizers of these educational centers, but I do not think it justifiable to encourage them, for in the first place, it is illogical and anti-economical to suppose that city children will ever disdain a city-wage to go to the country and work as field hands at much smaller wages and

p. 141

in an environment entirely different from that to which they are accustomed. In Mexico, as elsewhere, the exodus is from the country to the city, a thing to be deplored, and avoided if possible, but nevertheless a fact. I am not likely to be contradicted in my second objection, which is that the number of small crafts and industries taught-such as carpentry, blacksmithing, gardening, plumbing, baking, photography, dressmaking, embroidery, etc.—is infinitesimal compared to the actual number of crafts and industries which make up the web of a modern city such as the capital of Mexico, and with which the child should be acquainted in order to give his vocational awakening full opportunity. Finally, the small shops in which the pupils of such schools are taught are quite rudimentary, so that a child trained as a carpenter, blacksmith, or potter, is not equipped to work in factories which of course contain complex modern machinery, such as perforating saws, mechanical lathes, electric ovens, etc. The sphere of activity in these schools, no matter how good the instruction, is therefore limited; and the more so when the child is not made aware of the existence of other multiple urban activities which might yield him a livelihood.

The solution of this particular problem is to be found in the preliminary investigation of the means

p. 142

of livelihood which the city offers-the different ways of working, physically or intellectually, which are likely to yield either a daily wage or a monthly salary, or personal direct profit from the labor itself, forming with the information so obtained a progressive classification of activities, beginning with the most humble and going to the most complex. Upon such an investigation, a law could be projected which would obligate merchants, industrialists, constructors, and, in general, all employers according to the classification mentioned above to permit the school children to observe, at certain intervals and in detail, the working of their establishments. The factories and workshops of the nation should be automatically included in such a measure, heretofore untried in the city schools. After having been educated under a system with provisions such as these, the children would leave the schools knowing at least the ways of making a living in the city and how these are to be chosen to advantage, but most of all they will have had a chance to find out which of these holds the greatest attraction for them.

RURAL EDUCATION

If the economic factor is important in city education, it is even more so in rural; and its aspects

p. 143

are, to a certain extent, similar. The sources of life to be found in the fields fall into two great groups: (1) agricultural products which directly (vegetable) or indirectly (animal) reward man's cultivation of the soil; (2) raw materials (animal, vegetable, or mineral) found in rural Mexico, which can be transformed into industrial objects for domestic use or for sale in national or foreign markets.

The enormous area of our country and its small population, as well as the richness and profusion of raw materials at hand, might lead us to suppose that the rural inhabitants of Mexico live in a prosperous and enviable condition of physical and intellectual well-being. Quite the contrary, however, for the misery in which our great social majority lives is alarming, and its intellectual plane should be classed as primitive. This abnormal situation is due to the simple fact that the rural inhabitants have been confronted by great obstacles and have not known how to conquer them and therefore have not made satisfactory use of the natural resources within their grasp, to obtain from them the primary or secondary products which are the logical sources of life in whatever region they may live. In the first place, the uneven distribution of land ownership, and therefore of its products, has been the cause of grave economic problems, for the great

p. 144

majority of rural inhabitants has never possessed lands nor utilized them directly, nor enjoyed their products, but has been subject to life within a very small compass. This situation is being altered slowly through establishment of a system of small land-owning.

The second great obstacle which rural inhabitants encounter is a geographic, climatic one, for a large proportion of the land in our country, and especially that part owned by small proprietors, lacks irrigation and suffers the irregularities of the seasons, so that its yield is scanty or the crop a failure if it does not rain at the right time or the frosts are early. This situation could be relieved by the organizing of co-operative associations which would collectively construct dams, wells, and other channels for scientific irrigation.

The third and chief difficulty in which the rural inhabitants find themselves is their own ignorance of the utilizable elements in their native territories, and of the means for making the necessary industrial transformation of those which they do know. Furthermore, even when they do own lands and waters, they generally cannot secure the corresponding benefit, for they have not the agricultural education necessary for the proper cultivation and selections of animal and plant breeds. Furthermore, be-

p. 145

cause they have always done so, they continue raising maize almost exclusively, and this, in spite of the fact that in many regions it is in reality the least profitable of crops.

Education seems the answer to all these problems. Rural education should be more elemental and of shorter duration, so far as the purely scholastic end of it goes; whereas special attention should be given to agricultural and industrial teaching, so that the inhabitants may learn to take fullest advantage of those raw materials within their reach and to increase their crops, both in quantity and in quality. To impart rural education properly, the agricultural territories should be subdivided or classified according to geological formation, altitude, longitude, latitude, temperature, etc., and since the natural resources to be utilized vary greatly with these factors, the instruction about them should vary according to carefully mapped out zones.

Another difficulty: how to distribute efficiently this theoretical agricultural-industrial education among millions of rural inhabitants? Where to find teachers for the thousands or tens of thousands of rural schools set apart for this labor of redemption and social reconstruction? The teachers trained in the national or state normal schools

p. 146

would hardly be equipped for such a task, for teachers graduate from urban centers cannot possibly know, objectively and by experiment, the geographical, geological, agricultural, botanical, and zoölogical conditions of those regions to which they might later be sent; and they would therefore be unable to teach anything beyond the purely abstract knowledge which they themselves had received in the city, leaving their pupils still without instruction on how to cultivate and improve their lands and cattle, and how to effect the industrial and salable transformation of the natural elements available in each region. Further, no graduate of a national or state normal school would be willing to go to a rural school for a daily salary of from two to four pesos, which is a laborer's wage in the city. Socially, also, the teacher, molded to urban life, accustomed to city clothing, city food, lodging, and amusements, could hardly adapt himself satisfactorily to the rude life of a peasant; and, in the last analysis, he would be effecting his own retrogression by so doing.**

COAST REGION EDUCATION

One of the most serious, and not generally recognized, obstacles in the path of the normal development of our people is their under-nourishment; this is the fundamental cause of the exaggerated rates of mortality and the relative lack of dynamic impulse which can be observed in their activities.

We have already referred to the many ways of living open to both urban and rural inhabitants. Especially interesting are the organic resources of the coast regions, and the industries derived therefrom. We shall not discuss here the dietetic value of fish, it being enough to state that it is almost equal to beef and that it is the basic food for hundreds of millions of men all over the world and a

p. 151

source of wealth in many countries, even in some whose coasts are not very extensive. For example, there are the British Isles, whose statistics on the fishing industry for the year I905, before the use of the motor-boat, now considered almost indispensable, are especially worthy of attention. The fishing industry for that year netted Great Britain over 100,000,000 pesos and gave employment to 110,000 people. The treasury spent only $312,000 in the administration of the national fisheries and $148,000 in scientifically fostering the development of this industry.

To such figures Mexico presents a dismaying contrast. Our coasts are exaggeratedly extensive 9,000 kilometers. Along them can be found a great variety and number of species, especially those that fall into the three usual classifications: coast fish and sea food, shallow water open-sea fish, and deep water open-sea fish. Our coast population has all the ability and the necessary daring of any seagoing people. These antecedents would lead one to expect at least that fish or sea food would occupy a fairly important place on our menu, since it might be considered too much to expect its exportation. Instead, one finds that fresh fish is a rich man's dish, and this in season only; salt and canned fish is imported from foreign countries and never comes

p. 152

from our own coasts, and it is of course sold at prices way beyond the average reach.

The defective means of transportation from coast to interior explain this disastrous situation to a great extent, but there is another and more fundamental cause. We do not know the modern and efficient methods of the industry; we do not know how to fish, how to preserve the fish either in cans or salt, and we do not properly pack that which is consumed fresh. We make no use at all of the potential riches derived from the industry, such as tortoise-shell, pearl, coral, mother of pearl, or sea vegetable products. We have not even attempted to create markets for coast products in the interior of the country, let alone export them. In a word, we do not know how to make practical use of the inexhaustible possibilities in 9,000 kilometers of coast whose only limit is the open sea.

Here, again, the problem is educational, and its solution consists merely in teaching the coast population the most practical and efficient means of obtaining the maximum production from sea sources. When this has been procured, our national alimentation will have been bettered at least I00 per cent, and this would of course mean the satisfactory organic development of our people and greater profits on its labors. The now flourishing

p. 153

physical and economic development of England, Sweden, Norway, Japan, and other fish-eating peoples should seem ample foundation for our optimism in this respect.

The logical course of action would be to select several coast zones, representative of our littoral regions, and to establish in them educational centers which, besides giving the theoretic knowledge which is equally valuable in city, field, or coast, would teach the coast inhabitants the most modern and efficient fishery methods, in which particular instruction the Secretariat of Agriculture should collaborate, and in which the Secretariat of Industry might profitably assist in teaching the best industrial use of sea products. The Department of Health should in its turn send specialists effectively to fight the malarial fever, that terrible coast plague, and to introduce hygienic and preventive habits. The Secretariat of Industry and Commerce should then localize markets for sea products and make their transportation easier. The Secretariat of Finance (Hacienda) should help by projecting fiscal means of protecting and encouraging new and old sea industries. Finally, the projected Secretariat of Population would co-ordinate and make effective the studies and conclusions at which it might have arrived.

p. 154

I have had the honor of collaborating in some of the educational experiments which have been made in Mexico, and these conclusions are the result of my investigations. Above all, I would repeat the importance of the economic factor in Mexican education as a fundamental in the success or failure of whatever project is undertaken, and derived from this factor, the geographical one. For Mexico, as I have often said, is a heterogeneous country and should have its own special and peculiar system adapted to its many different needs, which can be gauged scientifically. Educationally, one must minister to each element as each element demands, in order to effect, paradoxically enough, a real unity, by lifting them all to the same plane of civilization. And by civilization I do not mean merely teaching the Indian how to read. I mean teaching him that he walks on rich soil and that there is a world around him. Throughout four hundred years he has stagnated miserably and has not even known it. He has not seen the failure of his efforts. He thinks he leads a normal existence; education will make him see that he can lead a better one and how he can do so.

Of as much or more interest to the American people than European or Asiatic politics, is the Mexican situation. Indeed, Mexico is a faithful mirror of the international aspirations and social needs of nearly all the countries from the Rio Grande to the Straits of Magellan, and as such it is valuable to the United States. It is also the very sensitive thermometer which registers to a degree the surges which successively warm and cool the attitude of the southern countries toward the United States. Finally, Mexico promises the brightest future to American industry, for it is now undergoing a most interesting economic-social change, which is an increase in the number of consumers of modern merchandise from three or four million to fifteen million. By raising the level of mass culture, chiefly of the Indians, their physical and mental wants, at present quite elemental and primitive, are automatically multiplied and intensified.

The same holds true for the rest of Latin Ameri-

p. 156

ca. The approximate twenty or thirty million consumers will in the near future become one hundred million. They will be people who have acquired and developed the demand for modern life and comfort, if the means to achieve such an end are opportunely taken. What country would be likely to benefit by this new enormous market? Certainly that producer-nation which possesses or has gained not only the good will of the respective Latin-American governments but, what is far more important, a foreseeing and ample comprehension of the needs, aspirations, and tendencies of those future buyers. It must be kept in mind that the Mexicans are the continerital touchstone. So much for the economic side of the matter.

As for the spiritual, sentimental, social, or any other, point of view, such understanding is even more vital. Let us consider, for example, the entirely different attitudes of Americans toward Mexicans and toward Canadians. The American esteems the Canadian, not only because cordial diplomatic and economic bonds unite the two governments, but also because the Canadian is of the same race, speech, and culture. The American accepts the Canadian on his own mental footing. On the other hand, he ignores or despises the Mexican, of whom he often has a distorted or vague idea; a

p. 157

circumstance due partly to the fact that the majority of the Mexicans who go to the United States are uncultured emigrants, competitors of American labor, seen only in their coarse industrial aspects.

It is about time for the American people to begin to acquire that accurate comprehension of Mexico which they lack in spite of the fact that, since the fall of the Diaz government some fifteen years ago, Mexico has been a pet topic of the American press. Much of the public ignorance is to be blamed on deliberately biased and malicious press reports sent out by unconditional and persistent enemies of Mexico, whose standards are fixed and whose paths are determined by uncontrolled prejudices and passions. They usually succeed in placing Mexico before the United States in a most unflattering light. On the other hand, the friends of the country, whether generous and unselfish or paid to do so, write of Mexico as a veritable promised land, a paradise of delicious fruits and rich metals and marvelous scenery. They picture Mexicans as simple and amiable creatures among whom the foreigner leads a comfortable and gently happy life. Of course not everything written about Mexico falls into one of these two classes. There have been accurate and sane reports, but at exceedingly rare intervals. There have existed, and

p. 158

do exist, irreproachable sources of information upon several aspects of Mexican life; these are the studies and investigations made by American scientists. Unfortunately, these studies, of interest to a limited number of specialists, hardly ever reach the general public-the man on the street.

Upon no more foundation than contradictory, deficient, and exaggerated reports, the American people can hardly be expected to form a satisfactory, just opinion of the complicated Mexican-American problem. I have asked American people chosen as types from different social strata for judgment on the relations between Mexico and the United States; and the average answer, to me the most interesting, was briefly this:

The American people and the Mexicans are two groups of human beings living under liberal and democratic laws in two countries exceptionally rich in natural resources. Yet the wellbeing of Americans of all classes is generally far superior to the corresponding situation of Mexicans. The United States has had only one civil war throughout its hundred and fifty years of independence, while Mexico and her sister republics have nearly all suffered incessant revolutions and the corresponding hardships during one hundred years of independence. It is hard to account for this difference between the lives of two peoples when conditions fundamental for development seem to have been identically favorable to both. For fifteen years, at least, the relations between Mexico and the United States have been unstable, hostile, and dangerous in spite of the fact

p. 159

that the two countries have many things in common and should be on the harmonious and satisfactory terms that their geographic positions and mutual economic advantage demand.

With the parallel here logically established, as a starting point to prove that the relations between Mexico and the United States are faulty, I shall make several observations, always with the understanding that I accept and appreciate such corrections as may be suggested.

Our international relations cannot be bettered effectively and permanently without giving proper attention to several considerations: (I) The Mexican people have been living under very bad conditions from time immemorial, and these must be improved. The natural resources in Mexico are practically virgin, and some development and utilization of them must be begun, for it is both antibiological and anti-social to expect a strong and rich people to be the cordial and friendly neighbor of a poor and weak one. (2) The American people ought to realize that if the Mexicans have not satisfactorily utilized the riches of their country and have lived in disastrous revolution and suffered great economic hardships, all of which has reflected unfavorably upon foreign investment, it has not been because of a temperamental and anti-cosmic

p. 160

idea of self-annihilation but because grave geographical, biological, historical, and racial obstacles have appeared constantly in the path of Mexican progress, often blocking it completely. The American people have had few if any difficulties to face when compared with the many and serious problems which have beset Mexico. These fundamental inequalities explain also the relative ease in governing the United States, compared to the same problem in Mexico. (3) American capital is less interested in the losses foreign investment suffered during past revolutions in Mexico, than in the future possibilities the country has to offer— future investments of the surplus billions which create annoying financial competition in the United States. It will not be long before gigantic investments will be made in Mexico; and they will be made as they are in the United States, that is, by making the same or similar concessions to the economic well-being of the country or community, though in Mexico, even with these concessions, the investment is likely to prove far more profitable. (4) The old idea of diplomacy based exclusively on economic advantage is losing prestige. In the near future, all diplomacy will be based on strictly scientific principles. Legations and embassies will function almost as official annexes of a university.

Let us make a brief geographical analysis of the United States and Mexico. It is often said that nature is wise and generous, for it supplies amply all human needs. The United States should, indeed, be deeply grateful to her; for besides making of its territory a rich and abundant natural warehouse, she bestowed upon it the facilities for taking effective advantage of its treasures. With Mexico, the loving Mother was equally lavish in the matter of initial boons, but perversely made the development of the potential resources quite difficult.

The first obstacle to human enterprise in Mexico is topography itself, that is, the lay of the land is exaggeratedly accidental. Mountains are numerous and plains scarce. This is not entirely a disadvantage. It means in certain instances a greater possibility of generating hydro-electric energy; it means a larger field of exposed mineral veins and

p. 162

strata; but it also means great difficulty of transportation of people and products from one region to another, and also many complications in agricultural development. In the United States, natural and manufactured products can be transported fairly easily and cheaply from camp and factory to consumer by rail. In Mexico there are thousands and thousands of square miles covered with cedar, mahogany, and sandalwood forests, but these precious woods cannot be lumbered because they are surrounded by rivers, mountains, and cliffs, and constructing railroads to these forests would mean multiplying the actual cost four or five times, due to the number of cuts, bridges, and tunnels that would be needed.

Certain special hats, made of palm leaves and bought by the millions in the Southern United States, come from a mountainous region whose borders touch the states of Pueblo and Oaxaca; and the only route to it is a trail so complicated by topographical accidents that the hats have to be carried by Indians in packs, for often not even horses or mules can thread their way along the rough trails in the sierra. The palm hat industry is a very important one, but even so, it could never justify nor compensate for the construction of a railroad in such a mountainous region. The relatively few rail-

Due to the numerous and complicated mountain ranges in Mexican territory, waterways are deficient. There are five or six rivers which might be termed navigable; the rest are swift and dangerous torrents which during the rainy season carry the water on the high table lands to the sea and which in the dry season are scarcely streams. Therefore, transportation by water is hardly plausible; whereas, in the United States the proportion of flat to rocky country is incomparably greater than in Mexico, and navigable rivers are numerous. Cotton, tobacco, cattle, and other products can be sent from the south and west to the north and east by railroads whose initial cost has been normal, or by rivers which were the gracious and spontaneous gifts of Mother Nature. The coal and machinery produced in northern and eastern mines and factories can be sent back by the same routes.

As to agriculture, Mexico again encounters difficulties due to the exaggerated mountainous nature of the land. Most of the water runs quickly from

Tradition sings the praises of the fertile Mexican tropics, but in reality their merits have been much exaggerated. Though the land is very rich, the unhealthy climate is so destructive of human life that, in the last analysis and according to statistics, production is mediocre. Until radical cures are found for tropical diseases, most of all. for malaria, it may be stated that agricultural production in the Mexican tropics and in the southern countries, where conditions are analogous, will not reach even normal, and many lives will still be lost.

One more, and most important, factor must be considered in Mexican agriculture. The variety of temperatures, due principally to the often-cited mountainous structure, makes that generalization or standardization essential to cultivation impossible. In the states of Puebla, Oaxaca, Chiapas, Michoacán, Jalisco, and many others the same soil produces sugar cane and wheat—that is, tropical products and cold-country vegetation are found

Botanically, it seems logical that wheat, barley, potatoes, and other plants should develop to better advantage in cold countries which are suited to their growth; whereas sugar cane, coffee, etc., would naturally grow better in those tropical regions where they originated. It would be valuable to investigate what plants really flourish in those mixed climates and which should be transplanted to other territories.

The only notable standardized regions in the general heterogeneity are Yucatan and the Laguna cotton country in Northern Mexico. Given the dry climate, the lime-shot soil, and the warm temperature of Yucatan, it can be seen how, after long and careful experiment, the hennequen or sisal, of which ropes and cables are made, was established as its most productive crop. Immediately, all other domestic vegetation was substituted by hennequen to such an extent that now Yucatan has to import even the most elemental necessities, such as industrial tools, cereal foods, and cattle. But on the other hand, this state produces more hennequen than any other place in the world, and it is literally criss-crossed with railroads; while its people live under much better conditions than those of the other states in the republic. From the point of view of political geography, one might make jocose, but justified, comment. The extensive plains and prairies of Argentina and North America-the wide horizons, the regular, monotonous surfaces—seem to create a spirit of tranquillity, of peaceful, anti-revolutionary content in the inhabitants; while the symbolic aspect of Mexican territory seems to be reflected in its people, whose lives parallel the conflict of mountains, the protest of fiery volcanoes, the sudden, violent torrents which tear trees from their roots and destroy entire villages. The very land is geologically both very new and very old, battling continually to secure place and equilibrium. Such broken country means secure shelter in every square yard of it for a wild animal or a bandit. That is why, traditionally, Mexico has always been pestered by one or more bands of political malcontents or out-and-out bandits who, in their mountain or forest retreats, are safe from all attack; an eloquent example is provided by the case

The tomato, without which no American luncheon table is complete, is also an Indian contribution, as is chocolate, justly called a beverage of the gods. Since Madero did not belong either to the middle or lower classes, his revolution became, surrounded as it was by difficulties, hardly more than a superficial movement. The embers of economic injustice and social discontent did not blaze up until later.

Logically and inevitably, then, arose Carranza, Obregón, Calles, and men of their stamp, who razed the old institutions to the ground to build an entirely new social edifice in place of these anachronisms. One cannot insist that this transformation is perfect, and one must admit that in its course it has suffered unnecessary evils and gone to reactionary extremes; but even these things have been the natural consequences of natural causes, and any people, emerging from a state similar to that in which we were, would have gone by the same route.

Let us examine closely the policies of American diplomacy during the long period of revolution. When Madero became president, the American representative was surprised by this dissolution of the trinity which had heretofore meant "Mexico" to the diplomatic corps, for though the martyr president had belonged to the conservative minori-

p. 177

ty by caste, his comrades in revolution had torn him away from this class and most of its ideals and wanted to make him completely like themselves. When President Madero was assassinated, the American diplomat informed Washington that the nation, to him still represented by the driftwood of the well-known trinity, was discontented under President Madero and had therefore sacrificed him to substitute some other executive. The American executive, however, sensed that somewhere the voice of the people, the middle and lower classes, had been muffled; and later investigation corroborated him, for it disclosed that the great and democratic Mexican majority loved Madero, who had been brought down by the conservative minorities and other elements, with foreign aid. Washington wavered, recognizing that here there was a real cause, and this cause, after bloody and bitter struggle, triumphed.

During the government of Carranza the American representative had to reconcile the old and hostile criterion, based on the definitely past traditional trinity, with the new order of things. Carranza, a man of many faults but withal a true progressive and a man of the people, wanted the United States to understand as far as possible the justice of his people's demands; while the conserva-

p. 178

tive minorities we have mentioned, the business men and the foreigners, played to make the representatives listen only to their own need and desires. Therefore, in spite of the evident good intention at Washington to arrive at a fair and democratic appreciation of our citation, the embassy's point of view prevailed; and this point of view was identical with that of the conservatives and reactionaries, though its effect was often fought and even nullified by the personal effort of President Wilson.

During the Obregón Administration, Ambassador Warren took a neutral and broad-minded stand; for, while he listened to the opinions of the conservatives and of the foreign business men, he also abandoned all diplomatic tradition and informed himself, as much as was within his power, upon the conditions and necessities of the social majorities, especially in their relation to international affairs, going for his information directly to original sources.

Of Ambassador Sheffield in his official capacity I know little, having been away from my country for over a year and being consequently somewhat out of touch with its diplomatic life.

But to return to our original theme, the Mexican government, throughout these last years, not satisfied with knowing the American people only

p. 179

through its diplomatic representatives and its business men, has sought and obtained more intimate relations. Broad-minded and unscheming scientists, artists, labor leaders, and newspaper men have been attracted to the country. Every year a number of Mexican youths are sent to the United States to school to study and understand the ideas and ideals of their colleagues. At the same time it has been made easy and attractive for American students to come to the Mexican National University, especially to the now famous summer sessions.

From the purely diplomatic point of view, Mexico has initiated a new policy in the United States and other countries, an excellent illustration of which is the present Mexican ambassador to Washington, who does not owe his position to political virtues, for he has always been outside of politics, and who furthermore has never given exclusive importance to the canons of traditional diplomacy. He is undoubtedly the first ambassador to really represent the Mexican people in the United States, and not merely the aristocrats and plutocrats, as most all his predecessors have done. He has ample perspective and integral knowledge of the social structure of our country; of its characteristics and tendencies; above all, wherein these concern or touch the United States. Furthermore,

p. 180

he is well aware of the social, economic, and political problems of the American people and can therefore always know when any of them affect Mexico, directly or indirectly. Finally, for some time there has existed in Mexican legations and embassies an attaché who represents exclusively the middle and lower classes and whose task it is to investigate the best means employed elsewhere to better the living conditions of those classes.

This is what Mexico has done to know the American people and make them know us. What, concretely, has been the corresponding work of the United States of America?

There are two phases to this subject: (I) the official judgments of the American government upon Mexico, and the sphere to which these have been limited; (2) the knowledge which unofficial American individuals or institutions may have of Mexico, and the circle in which this information has been distributed.

In the first place, Mexico, a country eminently rich in natural resources and raw materials, whose industrial production is of little importance, has always been considered the closest and most logical customer of American industries, and furthermore the most convenient and economical market for raw materials of the same industries. Also, Mexico

p. 181

has been the field for investing billions of dollars to make them produce more than they would in the United States. Upon such antecedents have our diplomatic relations been based. The American embassies have generally functioned only as agents of great international business. There never was any social contact, any sympathy of spirit, any altruism or human co-operation between the two; and therefore, even from a purely commercial point of view, this exchange was always bad or at best mediocre.

In Mexico, we repeat, there are four million buyers and sellers, whose business with the United States reaches an annual figure of 400,000,000 pesos; but there are also those other ten or twelve million who could also buy and sell approximately I,500,000,000 pesos annually, but who do not because, first of all, American business men have not attempted, privately or officially, to transform these millions of passive neighbors into active customers or salesmen. Relatively and in proportion, Wanamaker and Gimbel and merchants of their type, by sacrificing a few millions of profit, achieve a greater success and corresponding profit. They provide for the needs of their customers; and besides the exquisite courtesy and service, they have moderately priced restaurants, first-aid booths,

p. 182

restrooms, washrooms, telephone and telegraph service, etc.

International business is far from this goal. American interests have hardly ever willingly ceded some part of their profits for the economic and social betterment of the millions of Mexicans, who, as we have already said, could, if incorporated into even an elementary civilization, mean a new market and an increase of 400 per cent in the commercial balance between the two nations. The system followed has been solely that bad business policy of continual coolness and friction, not taking into consideration at all that most important of factors, the human factor, and the only one which means smooth, flexible, well-lubricated international relations. Most significant of all is the fact that the only reason for this policy is official custom and tradition.

Non-official investigations have been conducted by American institutions whose tendency is altruistic and disinterested. They have gained a knowledge of the human aspects of Mexican problems, and their influence could be of great value in effectively bettering the diplomatic relations between the two countries; so also could the Pan-American Union, more than anything a potential institution, whose work could be of as great value

p. 183

as that of the several expeditions and investigators idealistically working in the Mexican field.

The Rockefeller Foundation has collaborated with several Mexican administrations in the most noble task possible-that of saving thousands and millions of potential victims from the yellow fever.

The American Geographic Society, in its very important book The Land Problems of México by McBride, demonstrates objectively and scientifically that to better the economic conditions of the Mexican people it is fundamentally necessary to create small property, a conclusion which justifies the Mexican land and property laws, the spirit of which has been much misunderstood.

The Carnegie Institute intelligently investigates just what factors made the Maya race of Yucatan, one of the most civilized in the world; and we Mexicans now face the task of investigating just what factors brought about its present-day decadence and what means would lift it to civilization on a modern plane.

The Brookings School of Economics and Government has sent to Mexico intelligent investigators of economic relations between the two countries.

The Social Science Research Council of Chicago is about to begin, with the collaboration of Mexi-

p. 184

cans, an investigation of a transcendent problem, that of Mexican immigration • into the United States.

Finally, the Norman Wait Harris Memorial Foundation of the great University of Chicago has organized and is carrying out one of the most interesting projects which have been undertaken with reference to Mexican-American problems.

The list of institutions and persons who labor at this great task of achieving harmonious and fraternal relations between the United States and Mexico is a long one. The key to the situation is the material progress of Mexico, and direct or indirect co-operation toward this end is a definite and objective means of eliminating our eternal apple of discord—the economic differences between the two nations.

Unfortunately, the results of this noble and highly important work reach a limited circle and play a passive rôle in the official relations of American administrations toward Mexico. This fact is easily explained, for these generous and cultured institutions labor alone, privately, and the results of their investigations are generally one-sided though of high value as such.

In view of these circumstances and moved not only by scientific interest, but also and chiefly by

p. 185

that interest which my country arouses in me, I permit myself, in conclusion, to suggest: Let the many scientific institutions I have already named each choose a specialist, these specialists to form a great advisory body dedicated to the integration and compilation of the data procured by those institutions individually, in reference to Latin-American problems as related to the United States, beginning with the Mexicans since they are the most representative of the southern countries. Then, let the synthesis and conclusions reached in that work be published and circulated profusely through all the United States and all its social organizations.

These disinterested studies and investigations which have been made about Mexico, chiefly those on social sciences, would be of value to the Department of State at Washington, were the serene and impartial opinions contained therein allowed some part in forming a justified policy toward the southern countries. Further, since all American embassies and legations in our countries have a military attaché who studies problems pertaining to his science, and a commercial attaché whose duty it is to foster and aid purely material interchange, why not send also a scientific attaché to investigate that most interesting factor in international relations, the human factor; and thereby discover the reason

p. 186

for the social and cultural hostility between the Anglo-Saxon and Indo-Hispanic elements on this continent, and propose the remedy for such a situation.

When such a corps of representatives from all the intellectual institutions of the United States can show to the Department of State the conclusions at which it has been able to arrive, it is probable that these conclusions would begin to have some weight and influence in the American policy toward Latin-American countries. If these conclusions can be made to reach the American people, in popular printed form, a true approach and true unification of ideals shall have been begun for the people of this continent.
